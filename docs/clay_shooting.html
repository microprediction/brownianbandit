Clay Shooting

Equilibrium effort solving outstanding problems.

Peter Cotton · working draft, September 2026 · tex · numerics · output

Abstract

A prize for an unsolved problem is a clay pigeon: it is thrown up, several guns fire, only the first hit counts, and every other shell is spent. This paper closes the parameters of the Brownian stopping race so that the question “how much effort does a purse of size \(V\) call forth” has an answer. Running one approach to its optimal abandonment boundary generates the reduced form of the entry game exactly: an attempt succeeds with probability \(p\), costs \(p(1-p)V\) in expectation, and is worth \(Vp^{2}\), so the feasible reduced forms trace a single parabola and no optimally run attempt ever spends more than a quarter of the prize. With those costs, free entry is far less crowded than the pool: everybody attempting is an equilibrium only for \(n\le3\) or \(p\ge p^{*}(n)\approx\tfrac12(1+\sqrt{1-4/n})\), and in the long-odds limit the expected number of entrants falls to exactly two however many capable people exist. Persistence, by contrast, grows like \(\log V\), so purses buy guns faster than they buy stamina, and the philanthropist's optimal purse is a fixed multiple of the walk-away threshold, independent of total budget. Calibrating to the Millennium Prize Problems, the rival hazard supplies under a tenth of the impatience facing an attempt and the cash purse is about one percent of the prize that observed effort reveals: those races are run against cost and the clock, not against each other. Clay shooting is the right metaphor only once machine-cost attempts compress time-to-solution from decades to years.

1. Introduction

The Clay Mathematics Institute committed one million dollars to each of seven problems in May 2000. One has been solved. The purse is famous, and it is almost certainly irrelevant: the effort spent on those seven problems exceeds seven million dollars by orders of magnitude, and the one person who earned a purse declined it. Any model that treats the cash as the incentive gets the wrong answer. A model that takes the cash as a measuring rod can do better, because the same arithmetic that fails to explain the effort tells us what the effort reveals.

The machinery is a companion paper's. There, \(n\) researchers choose whether to attempt a prize problem; an attempt is a privately observed diffusion that wins on first passage to a target, costs a flow rate while it runs, and may be irreversibly abandoned. That paper supplies two ingredients and stops: a one-period entry game with an exact potential, and a closed-form abandonment boundary for a driftless attempt facing a constant rival hazard. It leaves \((p,k)\) — the success probability and expected cost of an attempt — as free parameters of the entry game, which is exactly what makes the entry game unfalsifiable.

This paper closes that loop and then spends the closure. Three results.

The reduced form is a parabola. An attempt run to its own optimal boundary is not free to have any \((p,k)\). In the driftless undiscounted case, \(k=p(1-p)V\) and the attempt is worth \(Vp^{2}\), identically (Proposition 1). The entry game therefore has one free parameter, not two, and the companion's profitability assumption \(k<Vp\) holds automatically. A corollary that a contest designer should know: expected spend per attempt is at most \(V/4\).

Free entry is thin. Substituting those costs, everybody attempting is an equilibrium for \(n\le3\) at any \(p\), and for \(n\ge4\) only when \(p\) exceeds a threshold \(p^{*}(n)\) that we compute in closed form and that rises to \(1\) like \(1-1/n\) (Proposition 2). In a large pool the interior equilibrium has expected entrants \(N^{*}(p)\) that is increasing in \(p\) with infimum exactly \(2\) (Proposition 3). Long odds do not summon a crowd. They summon two.

Depth is dearer than breadth. The continuation width bought by a purse grows like \(\sqrt V\) undiscounted and like \(\log V\) once discounting or rivalry bites, while entry responds at the margin of a linear comparison. A philanthropist splitting committed capital across identical problems should therefore set each purse at a fixed small multiple of the minimum viable purse — about \(2.25\) times it in the long-odds closed form, \(2.04\) times it in the exact numerical solve — and put every remaining dollar into more problems (Proposition 4).

Section 5 spends the closure on the Millennium problems. Three inversions: the rival hazard supplies between 5% and 16% of the effective impatience facing an attempt, so those races are run against cost and the clock; the purse is 0.4% to 3.3% of the prize that observed effort reveals; and the cash alone funds about three years of one person's diffusive travel. The clay-pigeon metaphor is therefore wrong for the Millennium problems as they have actually been attempted, and becomes right precisely when the cost of an attempt falls to machine prices, which raises the solution hazard and hands abandonment over to rivalry for the first time.

What is and is not claimed.

Propositions 1–4 are proved. Section 5 is a calibration under stated dials, not an estimation: the per-attempt success probability and the cost of a serious assault are stipulated, and the solution hazard is a Poisson rate estimated from a single event. Section 6 lists the modelling assumptions that mathematics violates, one of which — irreversibility of abandonment — it violates flatly.

2. The race that generates the entry game

Fix one researcher and one approach. While working, the researcher observes \(dX_{t}=\sigma\,dW_{t}\) with \(\sigma>0\), starting at \(x_{0}<b\); first passage to \(b\) is a solution worth \(V\); working costs \(c>0\) per unit time; abandonment is irreversible. Write \(d=b-x_{0}\) for the distance still to travel, and let \(\rho=r+h\) collect the discount rate and any constant rival solution hazard. The companion paper's boundary result is that the optimal policy continues on an interval \((a,b)\) of width

\[ L(V)=b-a=\begin{cases} \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\,\operatorname{arcosh}\!\left(1+\dfrac{\rho V}{c}\right), & \rho>0,\\[10pt] \sigma\sqrt{V/c}, & \rho=0,\end{cases} \]

and abandons at or below \(a\). Entry is worthwhile at all only if \(L(V)>d\). The reduced form of an attempt is the pair \(p\), the probability of solving before abandoning, and \(k\), the cost rate times the expected time spent — both computed without discounting, since these are the objects the one-period entry game consumes. The following identity is the point of this section.

Proposition 1 (Exact reduction). Let \(\rho=0\) and define the hardness index \(\zeta=d\sqrt c/\sigma\), so that \(\zeta^{2}=c\cdot d^{2}/\sigma^{2}\). If \(V\le\zeta^{2}\) the optimal policy abandons at once and the attempt is worthless. If \(V>\zeta^{2}\), then

\[ p=1-\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt V},\qquad k=p(1-p)\,V,\qquad Vp-k=Vp^{2}=\big(\sqrt V-\zeta\big)^{2}, \]

and \(Vp^{2}\) is the value of the attempt.

Proof. With \(\rho=0\) the boundary is \(a=b-L\), \(L=\sigma\sqrt{V/c}\), and \(x_{0}-a=L-d\). For driftless Brownian motion on the bounded interval \((a,b)\), optional stopping applied to \(X\) and to \(X_{t}^{2}-\sigma^{2}t\) at \(\tau=\tau_{a}\wedge\tau_{b}\) — legitimate because \(X\) is bounded on the interval and \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]<\infty\) — gives the two gambler's-ruin identities \(p=(x_{0}-a)/(b-a)=1-d/L\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]=(x_{0}-a)(b-x_{0})/\sigma^{2}=d(L-d)/\sigma^{2}\). Since \(L=\sigma\sqrt{V/c}\) we have \(d/L=\zeta/\sqrt V\), which is the first claim, and \(\sigma^{2}=cL^{2}/V\), so \(k=c\mathbb{E}[\tau]=cd(L-d)/\sigma^{2}=V(d/L)(1-d/L)=V(1-p)p\). Hence \(Vp-k=Vp^{2}=(\sqrt V-\zeta)^{2}\). That this expected net payoff equals the value follows because the policy in question is the optimal one; directly, the companion's closed form gives \(v(x_{0})=c(x_{0}-a)^{2}/\sigma^{2}=Vp^{2}\). Finally \(L>d\) is equivalent to \(V>\zeta^{2}\), and at or below that purse the value is zero. □

Three readings.

The minimum viable purse is a travel time.

\(V_{\min}=\zeta^{2}=c\,d^{2}/\sigma^{2}\), and \(d^{2}/\sigma^{2}\) is exactly the diffusive time for the state to cover the remaining distance \(d\). So the walk-away threshold is the cost rate times the diffusive traverse: a purse must cover an undirected crossing of the gap before anyone will start, and it must do so out of its own pocket, because at \(\rho=0\) nothing else discounts the effort.

The feasible reduced forms are one curve.

\((V,c,\sigma,d)\) enter \(p\), \(k/V\) and \(v/V\) only through the single ratio \(\zeta/\sqrt V=1-p\).

Corollary 1 (The entry game has one parameter). As \((V,c,\sigma,d)\) range over the positives with \(V>\zeta^{2}\), the attainable pairs \((p,k/V)\) are exactly the arc \(\{(p,p(1-p)):p\in(0,1)\}\). Consequently \(k<Vp\) always holds strictly, and \(k\le V/4\), with equality only at \(p=1/2\).

The bound is worth stating for its own sake: an attempt run to its own optimal boundary never spends, in expectation, more than a quarter of the prize, and spends that much only when it is a coin flip. Cheap long shots and near-certainties both spend little; the expensive attempts are the genuinely uncertain ones.

Discounting breaks the identity, not the method.

For \(\rho>0\) the width is no longer \(\sigma\sqrt{V/c}\), so \(k=c\,d(L-d)/\sigma^{2}\) still holds but the clean relation \(k=p(1-p)V\) does not. The honest statement is that the exact reduction belongs to the undiscounted benchmark, which is also the only case in which feeding undiscounted \((p,k)\) into an undiscounted entry game is internally consistent. With \(\rho>0\) both sides must be discounted together; we do not pursue that here, and every entry result below is a result about the \(\rho=0\) reduced form.

A verification.

A committed, seeded Euler simulation of the attempt — boundary location and increment law as its only inputs — reproduces the identity. At \(V=100\), \(c=\sigma=1\), \(d=4\), so \(\zeta=4\), \(L=10\) and \(p=0.6\), fifty thousand paths at \(dt=2\times10^{-3}\) give \(\hat p=0.6051\) with standard error \(0.0022\) and \(\hat k=24.05\) with standard error \(0.092\), against \(p=0.6\) and \(k=p(1-p)V=24\). The small upward bias in \(\hat p\) is Euler overshoot of the upper boundary, and it shrinks with \(dt\).

3. How many guns

Take \(n\) symmetric researchers facing one problem, each with the reduced form of Proposition 1 at common \(p\), each entering with probability \(q\). Ties among successes are broken uniformly and only one purse is paid. The companion's symmetric entry condition is that the gross return to entering against common probability \(q\), \(R(q)=V[1-(1-pq)^{n}]/(nq)\), be compared with \(k\): universal entry \(q=1\) is an equilibrium iff \(k\le R(1)\), and an interior equilibrium solves \(R(q)=k\).

3.1 When everybody attempts

Proposition 2 (Universal entry). Substitute \(k=p(1-p)V\) and write \(w=1-p\). Then \(q=1\) is a symmetric equilibrium if and only if \(\varphi_{n}(w):=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}w^{j}-nw\ \ge\ 0\). For \(n=2\) and \(n=3\) this holds at every \(p\), since \(\varphi_{2}(w)=p\) and \(\varphi_{3}(w)=p^{2}\). For \(n\ge4\) there is a unique \(w_{n}\in(0,1)\) with \(\varphi_{n}(w_{n})=0\), and \(q=1\) is an equilibrium exactly when \(p\ge p^{*}(n):=1-w_{n}\). Moreover \(w_{n}\) solves \(n\,w(1-w)=1-w^{n}\), so dropping the term \(w^{n}\) gives the closed form

\[ \hat p^{*}(n)=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4/n}}{2}=1-\frac1n-\frac1{n^{2}}+O(n^{-3}), \]

whose discriminant vanishes precisely at \(n=4\).

Proof. Universal entry requires \(p(1-p)V\le V[1-(1-p)^{n}]/n\), that is \(n\,p\,w\le 1-w^{n}=(1-w)\sum_{j<n}w^{j}=p\sum_{j<n}w^{j}\). Dividing by \(p>0\) gives the condition. Now \(\varphi_{n}'(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}jw^{j-1}-n\) is nondecreasing on \([0,1]\), so \(\varphi_{n}\) is convex there, with \(\varphi_{n}(0)=1>0\), \(\varphi_{n}(1)=0\), \(\varphi_{n}'(0)=1-n<0\) and \(\varphi_{n}'(1)=n(n-3)/2\). If \(n\le3\) then \(\varphi_{n}'(1)\le0\), and convexity forces \(\varphi_{n}'\le0\) throughout \([0,1]\); a nonincreasing function with \(\varphi_{n}(1)=0\) is nonnegative on \([0,1]\). If \(n\ge4\) then \(\varphi_{n}'(1)>0\), so \(\varphi_{n}<0\) just left of \(1\); combined with \(\varphi_{n}(0)>0\) and convexity, \(\varphi_{n}\) has exactly one zero \(w_{n}\) in \((0,1)\), is positive on \([0,w_{n})\) and negative on \((w_{n},1)\). Hence the condition holds iff \(w\le w_{n}\). The stated equation for \(w_{n}\) is \(\varphi_{n}=0\) multiplied by \(1-w\), and the closed form is the smaller root of \(nw(1-w)=1\) subtracted from one. □

The neglected term is \(w_{n}^{n}\approx n^{-n}\), so the closed form is not merely asymptotic but numerically exact very quickly. In the committed table it errs by \(8.6\times10^{-2}\) at \(n=4\), \(7.1\times10^{-4}\) at \(n=5\), \(3.7\times10^{-8}\) at \(n=8\), and by less than machine precision from \(n=20\) on.

3.2 The volume of a free-entry race

Proposition 3 (Free-entry volume). Let \(n\to\infty\) with expected entrants \(N=nq\) held fixed, so \(q\to0\). The interior symmetric equilibrium condition \(R(q)=k\) with \(k=p(1-p)V\) becomes, in the limit, \(1-e^{-y}=y(1-p)\) with \(y=pN\). Because \(G(y)=(1-e^{-y})/y\) decreases strictly from \(1\) to \(0\) on \((0,\infty)\), this has a unique root \(y^{*}(p)>0\) for every \(p\in(0,1)\), and

\[ N^{*}(p)=\frac{y^{*}(p)}{p},\qquad \mathbb{P}(\text{solved})=1-e^{-y^{*}(p)},\qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{solvers}]=y^{*}(p). \]

\(N^{*}\) is strictly increasing in \(p\), with \(N^{*}(p)=2+\tfrac43p+O(p^{2})\) as \(p\downarrow0\) and \(N^{*}(p)\to\infty\) as \(p\uparrow1\), so \(\inf_{p}N^{*}=2\). Aggregate expected expenditure equals the expected purse payout: \(N^{*}k=V(1-e^{-y^{*}})\).

Proof. \(R(q)=k\) reads \(V[1-(1-pq)^{n}]=nqk=Np(1-p)V\), and \((1-pN/n)^{n}\to e^{-pN}\), giving the limit equation. \(G\) is strictly decreasing with \(G(0^{+})=1\), \(G(\infty)=0\), so the root is unique and \(y^{*}\) is strictly increasing in \(p\). Parametrise by \(y\), with \(p=1-G(y)\) and \(N=y/(1-G(y))\). Then \(dN/dy=[(1-G)+yG']/(1-G)^{2}\) and \((1-G)+yG'=\psi(y)/y\) with \(\psi(y)=y-2+e^{-y}(2+y)\), where \(\psi(0)=0\) and \(\psi'(y)=1-e^{-y}(1+y)>0\) for \(y>0\) because \(e^{y}>1+y\). So \(\psi>0\), \(N\) is strictly increasing along the parametrisation, and hence \(N^{*}\) is strictly increasing in \(p\). Expanding \(G(y)=1-y/2+y^{2}/6-\dots\) gives \(y^{*}=2p+\tfrac43p^{2}+O(p^{3})\) and therefore \(N^{*}=2+\tfrac43p+O(p^{2})\). As \(p\uparrow1\), \(G(y^{*})\to0\) forces \(y^{*}\to\infty\) and \(N^{*}\ge y^{*}\to\infty\). Finally \(N^{*}k=N^{*}p(1-p)V=y^{*}(1-p)V=(1-e^{-y^{*}})V\), which is the expected payout. □

Proposition 3 is the paper's most compressible finding. At long odds, free entry produces two attempts in expectation, no matter how many people could have attempted. The two-researcher case of the companion paper reaches the same number from the other direction: its symmetric mixed solution is \(q=(2/p)(1-k/(Vp))=2\), which truncates to \(q=1\), so both enter and \(N=2\) exactly. The infimum is attained in the limit, not at any \(p\), and rent dissipation is complete at every \(p\): the pool spends the purse.

4. Breadth and depth

Money can buy two things: more attempts, or longer ones. They have very different prices.

Depth is logarithmic.

Persistence is the continuation width. Since \(\operatorname{arcosh}(1+z)\sim\log(2z)\) for large \(z\), \(L(V)\approx\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\log(2\rho V/c)\) when \(\rho V\gg c\), and only \(\sigma\sqrt{V/c}\) at \(\rho=0\). In units where \(c=\sigma=1\) and \(\rho=0.05\), multiplying the purse by \(100\) multiplies the continuation width by \(7.87\); by \(10{,}000\), only by \(21.9\). Under \(\rho=0\) the same multiples give \(10\) and \(100\). Either way the marginal dollar buys steeply less stamina, and the more competition there is (the larger \(\rho\)), the flatter the curve.

Breadth is cheaper per dollar.

Entry responds through \(p(V)=1-\zeta/\sqrt V\) and then through \(N^{*}(p)\), but the decisive asymmetry is that a second problem is bought outright at price \(V\), while a second unit of persistence on one problem is bought at an exponentially rising price. That comparison has a clean consequence.

Proposition 4 (Optimal purse splitting). A funder commits capital \(B\), fully escrowed, to identical problems of hardness \(\zeta\), choosing the number \(M\) of problems and posting \(V=B/M\) on each; free entry then determines each problem's solve probability by Proposition 3. Maximising the expected number solved, \(\Psi(M)=M(1-e^{-y^{*}(p(B/M))})\) with \(p(V)=(1-\zeta/\sqrt V)_{+}\), in the long-odds regime where \(1-e^{-y^{*}(p)}=2p+O(p^{2})\) gives

\[ M^{*}=\frac{4B}{9\zeta^{2}},\qquad V^{*}=\frac94\zeta^{2}=\frac94 V_{\min},\qquad \Psi(M^{*})=\frac{8B}{27\zeta^{2}}, \]

so the optimal purse is a fixed multiple of the minimum viable purse, independent of \(B\), and two thirds of funded problems are solved.

Proof. With \(p(B/M)=1-\zeta\sqrt{M/B}\) and the long-odds approximation, \(\Psi(M)\approx2M-2\zeta M^{3/2}/\sqrt B\). Differentiating, \(2-3\zeta M^{1/2}/\sqrt B=0\) gives \(\sqrt{M^{*}}=2\sqrt B/(3\zeta)\), hence \(M^{*}=4B/(9\zeta^{2})\) and \(V^{*}=B/M^{*}=\tfrac94\zeta^{2}\). Substituting, \(\Psi=8B/(9\zeta^{2})-16B/(27\zeta^{2})=8B/(27\zeta^{2})\), and \(p(V^{*})=1-\zeta/(\tfrac32\zeta)=\tfrac13\), so the per-problem solve probability is \(2p=\tfrac23\). □

The approximation is doing real work and should be checked, since \(p=1/3\) is not long odds. Solving the exact problem numerically at \(B=100\zeta^{2}\) over integer \(M\) returns \(M=49\), a purse of \(2.04\,V_{\min}\), \(p=0.300\), and \(26.1\) problems solved in expectation against the closed form's \(M=44.4\), \(2.25\,V_{\min}\) and \(29.6\). The purse multiple is close; the harvest is overstated by 13%, because \(2p=0.600\) overstates the true solve probability \(1-e^{-y^{*}}=0.533\) at \(p=0.3\). The qualitative conclusion — set the purse just above the walk-away threshold, spend the rest on more problems — survives, and the mechanism behind it is worth naming: a purse below \(V_{\min}\) buys nothing at all, a purse far above it buys logarithmically little extra depth, and the free-entry response caps the crowd at two anyway.

Two caveats. Expected count solved is one objective among many; a funder who cares more about the hardest problem should not spread. And \(B\) is committed capital, not expected outlay — the escrow is what makes \(M\) the choice variable.

5. Clay

Seven problems were posted in May 2000 at one million dollars each. Perelman's preprints settled the Poincaré conjecture in 2002–2003; the prize was awarded in 2010 and declined. Six remain open as of September 2026. Everything below reads that record through Sections 2–4 with dials stated explicitly. None of it is an estimate of a structural parameter.

5.1 Is the prize a clay pigeon?

The companion paper's mechanism is that a rival's solution destroys the option, so the rival hazard \(h\) acts as extra discounting: an attempt faces \(\rho=r+h\). The natural diagnostic is therefore the share of effective impatience that competition supplies, \(h/(r+h)\). One solution in \(7\times26=182\) problem-years gives \(\hat h=0.0055\) per problem-year. One event is one event, and the sampling uncertainty is enormous: the exact Poisson interval for \(\hat h\) is \([1.4\times10^{-4},\,3.1\times10^{-2}]\) per problem-year. The conclusion survives it in a weak but useful sense — even at the upper endpoint, the rivalry share at \(r=0.05\) is \(0.38\), still under a half — and fails to survive it in the sense that the interval cannot rule out rivalry mattering an order of magnitude more than the point estimate says.

Read the dials as: three focused years of a senior mathematician at 200k–400k dollars per year all-in, and a per-attempt success probability between one in two hundred and one in fifty. The implied prize is 31M to 241M, so the one-million-dollar purse is between 0.4% and 3.3% of it — one percent in the central cell. The purse is a scoreboard. Equivalently, the model says the cash would have to rise by a factor of fifty to two hundred before it became the marginal consideration for anyone already inclined to attempt.

A second, cruder inversion says the same thing from the cost side. The minimum viable purse is \(V_{\min}=c\,d^{2}/\sigma^{2}\), the cost rate times the diffusive traverse of the remaining distance. At 300k dollars per year, a one-million-dollar purse funds a traverse of \(3.3\) years and no more. The Millennium problems have absorbed careers. Whatever is paying for those careers, it is not the escrow.

5.3 Two guns, and a long tail the model forbids

Proposition 3 predicts, at \(p\) of a percent, an expected \(N^{*}\approx2.01\) serious attempts per problem regardless of how many mathematicians could mount one, with a solve probability of about \(2p\): free entry buys exactly one extra attempt over the lone-researcher benchmark. The small number of publicly identified long-run attackers per Millennium problem is consistent with that. The very large number of amateur attempts is not, and the mismatch is informative rather than embarrassing: under these dials such attempts have \(D_{i}<0\) in the companion's entry condition, so they cannot be prize-seeking. They are either heterogeneity the symmetric model suppresses — a few people with much higher private \(p_{i}\) or much lower \(k_{i}\) — or consumption, in which case the relevant \(V\) is not a prize at all. Either way the homogeneous free-entry count describes the serious tail of the distribution and nothing else.

5.4 When clay shooting starts

The interesting parameter is not the purse. It is \(\zeta=d\sqrt c/\sigma\) and, through it, the hazard. An attempt run at machine cost lowers \(c\) and, if the search is genuinely broader, raises \(\sigma\); both push \(\zeta\) down, which raises \(p=1-\zeta/\sqrt V\), lowers \(V_{\min}=\zeta^{2}\), and raises \(N^{*}\). The composition of those effects is a rise in the solution hazard, and that is the switch.

The model's prediction is sharp about what changes and what does not. Doubling the purse does almost nothing, by Section 4. Cutting \(\zeta\) in half quarters the minimum viable purse and moves \(p\) from, say, \(0.01\) to \(0.505\) at fixed \(V\), which by Proposition 3 takes \(N^{*}\) from \(2.01\) to \(3.2\) and the solve probability from \(0.02\) to \(0.80\). Once expected time-to-solution falls from decades to a few years, \(h\) moves from \(0.005\) to order \(0.3\), and the rivalry share moves from \(0.10\) to \(0.87\). Only then is the purse a clay pigeon, and only then does the companion paper's coupled hazard equilibrium, rather than its single-agent boundary, do the explaining. The observable signature would be a burst of entry followed by rapid abandonment by all but the leaders — a pattern the human record on these problems does not contain, because in the human record nobody is racing anybody.

6. What this gets wrong about mathematics

The list matters more than usual here, because the calibration in Section 5 is only as good as the modelling choices behind it, and one of them is simply false.

  • Abandonment is not irreversible. Mathematicians shelve problems and come back to them decades later. The baseline's only control is an irreversible kill, which is the right primitive for a metered compute job and the wrong one for a person. The right home for the human version is the intensity extension of the companion paper, in which \(u=0\) is a pause rather than a death; that changes the boundary and, in particular, removes the sharp walk-away threshold that \(V_{\min}\) represents. Every statement here that leans on \(V_{\min}\) — including Proposition 4 — should be read as a statement about the committed-attempt reading.
  • Difficulty is common, not private. The product survival formula behind the rivalry share needs independence across researchers. In mathematics the dominant uncertainty is shared: whether the statement is true, whether the method can work at all. A rival's visible failure is evidence about your own prospects, which turns the game into one with filtering and correlated states, and there the rival hazard is no longer a deterministic function of calendar time.
  • Progress is partly public. Preprints, seminars and conference talks reveal rival states. That is a joint-state game, and the private-diffusion structure is a simplification chosen for tractability, not realism.
  • Winner-take-all is an approximation. Partial results are published, cited and rewarded; a lemma that survives into the eventual proof pays something. The all-or-nothing first-passage payoff overstates the waste and therefore, through Corollary 1, understates the effort a given prize should call forth.
  • Verification takes years. The Poincaré purse was paid seven years after the preprints. A claimed proof that later fails restarts the race, so the announcement is not an absorbing event, and the hazard conflates “someone claims” with “someone solves”.
  • The dials are stipulated. \(p\), \(k\) and \(r\) in Section 5 are assumptions. The inversions are arithmetic on them, and their only real content is robustness: to overturn the conclusion that the purse is a scoreboard, one needs \(p(1-p)V\) near one million dollars, which at \(k=1\)M requires \(p\) near \(1/2\).

7. Related work

Racing for a prize.

Loury (1979) and Lee and Wilde (1980) set the pattern: a flow of R&D spending buys a Poisson hazard of success, and free entry dissipates the rents. Dasgupta and Stiglitz (1980) added industrial structure, Reinganum (1981) the dynamic game, and Fudenberg, Gilbert, Stiglitz and Tirole (1983) preemption and leapfrogging. Harris and Vickers (1985, 1987) put the race on a state space with observable positions and studied who gives up. What we add to that tradition is a derived rather than assumed relation between the cost of an attempt and its success probability: in Proposition 1 the pair is not chosen, it falls out of running a diffusion to its own optimal boundary, and \(k=p(1-p)V\) is the constraint the Poisson-hazard formulations leave free.

Private progress and preemption.

Hopenhayn and Squintani (2011) is the closest neighbour: privately observed progress, an option to stop, and preemption. Bobtcheff, Bolte and Mariotti (2017) model the researcher's own tradeoff between publishing early and polishing under priority risk. Moscarini and Squintani (2010) and Keller, Rady and Cripps (2005) study experimentation when rivals' information is a motive to keep going. All of those keep the strategic loop closed and leave the cost-to-probability mapping in reduced form; we do the opposite, taking the loop as given by the companion paper and closing the mapping instead.

Contest design.

Tullock (1980) and Baye, Kovenock and de Vries (1996) price rent dissipation; Taylor (1995) and Che and Gale (2003) design research tournaments; Fullerton and McAfee (1999) auction entry to select contestants; Halac, Kartik and Liu (2017) design contests when the technology must be discovered. Proposition 4 is a design result of the same kind with an unusually crude instrument: the designer chooses only how many purses to post and how large, and the answer is a fixed multiple of the walk-away threshold.

Entry games and potentials.

Morgan, Orzen and Sefton (2012) and Fu, Jiao and Lu (2015) study endogenous entry with subsequent effort; the companion paper's fixed-attempt lottery and its exact potential sit in the tradition of Rosenthal (1973) and Monderer and Shapley (1996). Corollary 1 says which points of that game's parameter space a Brownian attempt can actually occupy.

Budgeted races.

The forward–backward architecture of the companion paper is borrowed from the budgeted Brownian race, where a single controller prunes many paths under a path-time budget and is paid the terminal maximum. The two differ in what closes the loop: there an optimality condition with a shadow price, here a consistency condition between each researcher's stopping rule and the hazard the others generate. Its concavity certificates therefore say nothing about equilibrium here, a point the companion paper makes and this one inherits.

8. Closing

The arithmetic of this paper is elementary and its conclusion is deflationary. Closing the parameters of a Brownian prize race pins the cost of an attempt to its success probability, and that single constraint is enough to show that free entry is thin, that persistence is expensive, and that a purse should be set just above the threshold at which anyone will start. Applied to the most famous prizes in mathematics, the same arithmetic says that those races are not races: the hazard a rival imposes is a tenth of the impatience an attempt faces, and the cash is a hundredth of the prize the effort reveals.

That makes the interesting question a forecast rather than a measurement. The model localises the whole of the change in one index, \(\zeta=d\sqrt c/\sigma\), and says that when the cost of an attempt falls to machine prices the rivalry share flips from a tenth to nine tenths. At that point the strategic fixed point of the companion paper stops being an elegant characterisation and starts being the object one has to compute. Three things would have to be settled first: existence and selection for the coupled equilibrium, a convergence guarantee for its iteration, and an honest treatment of common difficulty, which is the assumption mathematics violates most and which no calendar-time hazard can absorb.
