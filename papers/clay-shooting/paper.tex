\begin{abstract}
\noindent A Brownian attempt on a prize problem has a price list. Running
one to an abandonment floor buys success probability $p$ at expected cost
$k(p)=\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)$, where $\zeta^{2}$ is the cost of a diffusive
traverse of the remaining distance; the identity holds for every floor, so
an attempt's cost and success rate pin $\zeta^{2}$ with no appeal to
optimality, discounting, or the size of the prize. Sizing the attempt
against an effective reward $A$ gives $p=1-\zeta/\sqrt A$, $k=p(1-p)A$ and
value $(\sqrt A-\zeta)^{2}$; in a lottery for one prize $A$ is the shared
reward, not the purse, which is where a fixed-attempt entry game misprices
competition. Closing that loop, the symmetric equilibrium attempt size is
the unique root of $p=1-\zeta/\sqrt{A(p)}$, and in a large pool it
collapses to $G(y^{*})=V_{e}/V$, where $G(y)=(1-e^{-y})/y$, $y^{*}$ is the
expected number of solvers and $V_{e}$ the purse at which a lone
researcher breaks even: the effective reward is competed down to $V_{e}$
exactly, rents dissipate completely, and the number of \emph{entrants} is
not pinned at all. A funder splitting escrowed capital should post purses
of $(y^{*}+\tfrac12)V_{e}=1.7564\,V_{e}$ where $e^{y}=1+2y$, solving
$71.5\%$ of funded problems. The reward can exceed the walk-away threshold
by only $(2-p)/(1-p)$ times the cost of one attempt, so whether a cash
purse matters is governed by its ratio to twice that cost and not to the
prize --- which is why nothing here identifies the Millennium purses'
role, and why this draft withdraws the claim that they are scoreboards.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Clay Mathematics Institute committed one million dollars to each of
seven problems in May 2000 \citep{clay2000}; one has been solved, and the
person entitled to the purse refused it. That refusal is the most
informative single fact anyone has about the reward structure of famous
mathematics, and what it says is that the reward is not denominated in the
purse. It does not say by how much, and neither does this paper.

What this paper does is price the attempt. A companion note
\citep{cotton2026races} sets up a prize race: researchers choose whether
to attempt a problem, an attempt is a privately observed diffusion that
wins on first passage to a target, costs a flow rate while it runs, and
may be irreversibly abandoned. It leaves the success probability $p$ and
expected cost $k$ of an attempt as free parameters of its entry game.
Here they are derived, and the derivation turns out to constrain the game
far more than the first draft of this paper recognised.

\paragraph{Three results.}
First, a \emph{price list} (Section~\ref{sec:price}). For an attempt run
between a target at distance $d$ and an abandonment floor,
\begin{equation}
k(p)=\zeta^{2}\,\frac{p}{1-p},
\qquad
\zeta^{2}=\frac{c\,d^{2}}{\sigma^{2}},
\label{eq:intro-price}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta^{2}$ is the cost rate times the diffusive traverse of the
distance still to go. This holds for \emph{every} floor, optimal or not,
at any discount rate, so an observed $(k,p)$ pair identifies $\zeta^{2}$
outright. Sizing the attempt optimally against an effective reward $A$
gives $p=1-\zeta/\sqrt A$, $k=p(1-p)A$ and value
$(\sqrt A-\zeta)^{2}$. A corollary that matters later: the reward can
exceed the walk-away threshold $\zeta^{2}$ by only $(2-p)/(1-p)$ times the
cost of a single attempt, which is between two and three times it over
most of the range.

Second, an \emph{equilibrium in attempt sizes} (Section~\ref{sec:eq}).
In a lottery for one prize the reward conditional on success is shared,
$A_{i}=V\,\E[(1+K_{-i})^{-1}]<V$, so an attempt sized against the full
purse is too long. The symmetric equilibrium is the unique root of
$p=1-\zeta/\sqrt{A(p)}$, and in a large pool it becomes
\begin{equation}
G(y^{*})=\frac{V_{e}}{V},
\qquad
G(y)=\frac{1-e^{-y}}{y},
\label{eq:intro-eq}
\end{equation}
with $y^{*}$ the expected number of solvers and $V_{e}$ the purse at which
a lone researcher exactly breaks even. The effective reward is competed
down to $V_{e}$ exactly and rents dissipate completely. What is pinned is
the number of \emph{solutions}; the number of entrants diverges as the
setup cost vanishes.

Third, an \emph{exact purse design} (Section~\ref{sec:purse}). A funder
splitting escrowed capital $B$ across identical problems maximises the
expected number solved at
\begin{equation}
e^{y}=1+2y,
\qquad
\frac{V^{*}}{V_{e}}=y^{*}+\tfrac12=1.75643121,
\qquad
\PP(\text{solved})=0.71533186,
\end{equation}
independent of $B$. The budget chooses the number of problems; the purse
is a fixed multiple of the break-even purse.

\paragraph{Corrections to the first draft.}
A referee found four substantive errors, and correcting them changes the
paper's conclusions, not just its wording. They are recorded here rather
than buried.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The reduction $k=p(1-p)V$ was applied inside a competitive entry
game, where the solo-optimal width is not a best response. It is
reinstated as $k=p(1-p)A$ against the \emph{effective} reward, and
Section~\ref{sec:eq} closes the resulting loop. The referee's counterexample
is reproduced in Section~\ref{sec:committed}.
\item ``Free entry produces two attempts'' is withdrawn. Even in the
committed-width game the claim required selecting the symmetric mixed
equilibrium: for $p<2-\sqrt2$ any three designated entrants form a pure
equilibrium, and the companion paper's own potential selects three. At
fixed $n$ the mixed equilibrium gives $2n/(n-1)$, not $2$. Under
endogenous widths the entrant count is not pinned at all.
\item ``Doubling the purse does almost nothing'' is withdrawn: it
contradicts $p=1-\zeta/\sqrt V$, under which
$dp/d\log V=(1-p)/2$ and doubling the purse moves $p$ from $0.01$ to
$0.29996$. The logarithmic-width result applies far above threshold with
positive discounting, which is not where the argument was made.
\item The Millennium ``revealed prize'' tables are withdrawn along with
the conclusion that the purses are scoreboards. The dials were stipulated,
so nothing was estimated; and the diagnostic used --- purse over prize ---
is the wrong ratio, because the model's own arithmetic keeps the surplus
small in level whenever attempts are long shots. Section~\ref{sec:clay}
now says what can and cannot be said, which is less.
\end{enumerate}
Two smaller repairs: the exposure for the solution hazard is at most
$6\times26+3=159$ problem-years, not $7\times26$, since the solved
problem stopped being exposed in 2003; and the old robustness sentence was
arithmetically impossible, since $k\le V/4$ means a \$1M purse can
rationalise an attempt costing at most \$250k.

\paragraph{Scope.}
Sections~\ref{sec:price}--\ref{sec:purse} are proved for the
\emph{lottery} allocation of the companion paper --- all entrants pay to
completion, one winner is drawn among the successes --- with
undiscounted, driftless attempts. The first-solution race, in which costs
cease when a rival announces and the rival hazard is a function of
calendar time, is not solved here; it is the companion's coupled system
and it remains open. Section~\ref{sec:clay} contains no estimation.

\section{The price of a success probability}
\label{sec:price}

Fix one researcher and one approach. While working, the researcher
observes $dX_{t}=\sigma\,dW_{t}$ with $\sigma>0$, starting at $x_{0}$;
first passage to a target $b>x_{0}$ is a solution; working costs $c>0$ per
unit time; abandonment at a floor $a<x_{0}$ is irreversible. Write
$d=b-x_{0}$ for the distance still to travel and $L=b-a$ for the
continuation width, and define the \emph{hardness index}
\begin{equation}
\zeta^{2}=\frac{c\,d^{2}}{\sigma^{2}},
\end{equation}
the cost rate times $d^{2}/\sigma^{2}$, which is exactly the diffusive
time for the state to cover the remaining distance.

\begin{proposition}[The price list]\label{prop:price}
For any width $L>d$, the attempt's undiscounted success probability and
expected cost are
\begin{equation}
p=1-\frac{d}{L},
\qquad
k=\frac{c\,d\,(L-d)}{\sigma^{2}}=\zeta^{2}\,\frac{p}{1-p}.
\label{eq:price}
\end{equation}
Hence $k(\cdot)$ is a strictly increasing, strictly convex function of $p$
on $[0,1)$ with $k(0)=0$ and $k(p)\to\infty$ as $p\to1$; and an observed
pair $(k,p)$ determines
\begin{equation}
\zeta^{2}=k\,\frac{1-p}{p}
\label{eq:identify}
\end{equation}
with no reference to the prize, the discount rate, the rival hazard, or
whether $L$ was chosen optimally.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Optional stopping applied to $X$ and to $X_{t}^{2}-\sigma^{2}t$ at
$\tau=\tau_{a}\wedge\tau_{b}$ --- legitimate because $X$ is bounded on
$(a,b)$ and $\E[\tau]<\infty$ --- gives the gambler's-ruin identities
$p=(x_{0}-a)/(b-a)=1-d/L$ and
$\E[\tau]=(x_{0}-a)(b-x_{0})/\sigma^{2}=d(L-d)/\sigma^{2}$, so
$k=c\E[\tau]$. Substituting $L=d/(1-p)$ gives
$k=c\,d^{2}p/\big(\sigma^{2}(1-p)\big)=\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)$. Convexity and the
limits are immediate, and \eqref{eq:identify} is \eqref{eq:price}
inverted.
\end{proof}

Everything else in the paper is an optimisation against this price list.

\begin{corollary}[Optimal attempt size]\label{cor:size}
Let $A>0$ be the expected reward conditional on solving. The attempt
maximising $Ap-k(p)$ over $p\in[0,1)$ has
\begin{equation}
p=\Big(1-\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt A}\Big)_{+},
\qquad
k=p(1-p)A,
\qquad
\text{value}=Ap^{2}=\big(\sqrt A-\zeta\big)_{+}^{2},
\label{eq:size}
\end{equation}
and the attempt is worth mounting exactly when $A>\zeta^{2}$. Expected
spend never exceeds $A/4$, with equality only at $p=1/2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$Ap-\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)$ has derivative $A-\zeta^{2}/(1-p)^{2}$, which
vanishes at $(1-p)^{2}=\zeta^{2}/A$ and is decreasing, so the stationary
point is the maximum when it lies in $(0,1)$, i.e.\ when $A>\zeta^{2}$;
otherwise the derivative is negative throughout and $p=0$. Substituting
$1-p=\zeta/\sqrt A$ into $k(p)$ gives $k=\zeta p\sqrt A=p(1-p)A$, and
$Ap-k=Ap^{2}=A(1-\zeta/\sqrt A)^{2}=(\sqrt A-\zeta)^{2}$. Finally
$k/A=p(1-p)\le1/4$.
\end{proof}

For a researcher with no rivals, $A=V$ and \eqref{eq:size} is the
first draft's Proposition~1. The whole of the trouble in that draft was
the step of holding $A=V$ inside a game where the prize is shared.

\begin{corollary}[The surplus is a small multiple of one attempt]
\label{cor:surplus}
If the attempt is optimally sized, the reward exceeds the walk-away
threshold by
\begin{equation}
A-\zeta^{2}=k\,\frac{2-p}{1-p}\;\in\;[2k,\infty),
\label{eq:surplus}
\end{equation}
which equals $2k$ in the long-odds limit and $3k$ at $p=1/2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$A=\zeta^{2}/(1-p)^{2}$, so
$A-\zeta^{2}=\zeta^{2}\big(1-(1-p)^{2}\big)/(1-p)^{2}
=\zeta^{2}p(2-p)/(1-p)^{2}$, and $k=\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)$.
\end{proof}

Corollary~\ref{cor:surplus} is the paper's most consequential line and
its least expected. However enormous the reward, if attempts are long
shots then the reward only just clears the threshold, and it clears it by
about twice the cost of a single attempt. A prize that looks like a
rounding error against the level of the reward can be a large fraction of
the surplus that makes attempting worthwhile at all. Section~\ref{sec:clay}
is where the first draft got this backwards.

\paragraph{A verification.}
A committed, seeded Euler simulation --- given only the boundary location
and the increment law --- reproduces \eqref{eq:size}. At $A=100$,
$c=\sigma=1$, $d=4$, so $\zeta=4$, $L=10$ and $p=0.6$, fifty thousand
paths at $dt=2\times10^{-3}$ give $\hat p=0.6051$ (s.e.\ $0.0022$) and
$\hat k=24.05$ (s.e.\ $0.092$) against $p=0.6$ and $k=p(1-p)A=24$. The
upward bias in $\hat p$ is Euler overshoot of the upper boundary and
shrinks with $dt$. The price identity \eqref{eq:price} is checked against
$k$ computed from the width at five widths, with residuals at $10^{-14}$.

\section{Equilibrium attempt size}
\label{sec:eq}

Take the companion paper's lottery: $n$ researchers attempt the same
problem, each pays for its own attempt to completion, and if several
succeed one is drawn uniformly for the prize $V$. Researcher $i$ chooses a
width, equivalently a success probability $p_{i}\in[0,1)$, and pays
$k(p_{i})$. Choosing $p_{i}=0$ is not entering. Successes are
independent.

Conditional on solving, researcher $i$'s expected reward is
\begin{equation}
A_{i}=V\,\E\Big[\frac{1}{1+K_{-i}}\Big]
=V\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{j\ne i}\big(1-p_{j}+p_{j}z\big)\,dz,
\label{eq:Ai}
\end{equation}
using $1/(m+1)=\int_{0}^{1}z^{m}dz$, where $K_{-i}$ counts successful
rivals. Payoffs are $A_{i}p_{i}-k(p_{i})$, so by
Corollary~\ref{cor:size} each researcher's best response is
$p_{i}=(1-\zeta/\sqrt{A_{i}})_{+}$ and depends on rivals only through the
scalar $A_{i}$. In the symmetric case \eqref{eq:Ai} collapses to
\begin{equation}
A(p)=V\,\frac{1-(1-p)^{n}}{np}.
\label{eq:Asym}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Symmetric equilibrium, finite pool]\label{prop:eqfinite}
$A(p)$ in \eqref{eq:Asym} is strictly decreasing in $p$ on $(0,1]$, with
$A(0^{+})=V$ and $A(1)=V/n$, and strictly decreasing in $n$. If
$V\le\zeta^{2}$ the only symmetric equilibrium is $p=0$. If
$V>\zeta^{2}$ there is a unique symmetric equilibrium $p_{n}\in(0,1)$,
namely the root of
\begin{equation}
p=1-\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{A(p)}},
\end{equation}
it is strictly decreasing in $n$, and each researcher earns
$A(p_{n})p_{n}^{2}>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $w=1-p$ and $f(p)=\big(1-w^{n}\big)/(np)$. Then
$\partial_{p}\big[(1-w^{n})/p\big]$ has the sign of
$g(p)=np w^{n-1}-1+w^{n}$, and $g(0)=0$ with
$g'(p)=-n(n-1)pw^{n-2}<0$, so $g<0$ and $A$ is strictly decreasing in
$p$. For monotonicity in $n$, $(n+1)(1-w^{n})-n(1-w^{n+1})
=1-(n+1)w^{n}+nw^{n+1}$ vanishes at $w=1$ with derivative
$n(n+1)w^{n-1}(w-1)<0$, hence is positive for $w<1$, giving
$A_{n}>A_{n+1}$.

Now $\Phi(p)=(1-\zeta/\sqrt{A(p)})_{+}-p$ is continuous and strictly
decreasing on $[0,1]$: where the positive part is active it is
$1-\zeta/\sqrt{A(p)}-p$ with $A$ strictly decreasing, and where it is
clamped it is $-p$.
$\Phi(0)=1-\zeta/\sqrt V$ and
$\Phi(1)=-\zeta\sqrt{n}/\sqrt V<0$. If $V\le\zeta^{2}$ then
$\Phi(0)\le0$ and $p=0$ is the unique root; note that $p=0$ is then
genuinely an equilibrium, since a deviator facing $A=V\le\zeta^{2}$ has
best response $0$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:size}. If $V>\zeta^{2}$ then
$\Phi(0)>0>\Phi(1)$ and strict monotonicity gives exactly one root, which
decreases in $n$ because $A_{n}$ does. The payoff is
Corollary~\ref{cor:size}'s value at $A=A(p_{n})>\zeta^{2}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Large pool]\label{prop:eqlimit}
Let $n\to\infty$ with $np\to y$. Then $A\to V\,G(y)$ with
$G(y)=(1-e^{-y})/y$, strictly decreasing from $1$ to $0$, and the
equilibrium condition becomes
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;G(y^{*})=\frac{\zeta^{2}}{V}\;}
\label{eq:limit}
\end{equation}
which has a unique positive root whenever $V>\zeta^{2}$. At that root the
expected number of solvers is $y^{*}$, the solve probability is
$1-e^{-y^{*}}$, the effective reward collapses to
$A^{*}=\zeta^{2}$, individual effort and payoff vanish, and aggregate
effort equals the expected payout,
\begin{equation}
\lim n\,k(p_{n})=y^{*}\zeta^{2}=V\big(1-e^{-y^{*}}\big).
\end{equation}
With a setup cost $K>0$ payable on entry, the same computation gives
\begin{equation}
G(y^{*})=\frac{V_{e}}{V},
\qquad
V_{e}=\big(\zeta+\sqrt K\big)^{2},
\qquad
p^{*}=\frac{\sqrt K}{\zeta+\sqrt K},
\qquad
N^{*}=y^{*}\,\frac{\zeta+\sqrt K}{\sqrt K},
\end{equation}
where $V_{e}$ is the purse at which a lone researcher exactly breaks even,
$A^{*}=V_{e}$, each attempt costs $k=\zeta\sqrt K$, and $N^{*}\to\infty$
as $K\downarrow0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$(1-p)^{n}=(1-y/n)^{n}\to e^{-y}$ gives $A\to VG(y)$, and
$p=1-\zeta/\sqrt A$ with $p\to0$ forces $\zeta/\sqrt{A^{*}}\to1$, i.e.\
$A^{*}=\zeta^{2}$ and hence \eqref{eq:limit}; uniqueness is strict
monotonicity of $G$. Then $k=p(1-p)A\to0$ and the payoff $Ap^{2}\to0$,
while $nk\to y\,A^{*}=y^{*}\zeta^{2}$, which equals
$V\,y^{*}G(y^{*})=V(1-e^{-y^{*}})$ by \eqref{eq:limit}. With a setup cost,
free entry requires $Ap^{2}=K$; substituting $p=1-\zeta/\sqrt A$ gives
$(\sqrt A-\zeta)^{2}=K$, so $\sqrt{A^{*}}=\zeta+\sqrt K$ and
$A^{*}=V_{e}$. Then $p^{*}=1-\zeta/(\zeta+\sqrt K)$,
$k=p^{*}(1-p^{*})A^{*}=\zeta\sqrt K$, and $N^{*}=y^{*}/p^{*}$. Finally
$V_{e}$ is the purse solving $(\sqrt V-\zeta)^{2}=K$, the lone
researcher's break-even condition in Corollary~\ref{cor:size}.
\end{proof}

The last column solves Proposition~\ref{prop:eqfinite} exactly at
$n=10^{4}$ and confirms the limit. A unilateral-deviation certificate
backs the fixed points: at three computed equilibria, a two-million-point
grid search over own success probability against the equilibrium rivals
improves on the equilibrium payoff by at most $4\times10^{-12}$, which is
the diagnostic the companion paper asks for and a real test, since the
grid does not know the closed-form best response.

Three readings.

\paragraph{What is pinned is solutions, not attempts.}
The expected number of solvers $y^{*}$ and the solve probability depend
only on $V/V_{e}$. The number of entrants does not: it is
$y^{*}(\zeta+\sqrt K)/\sqrt K$, which diverges as the setup cost
vanishes, because with free entry a great many researchers each buy a
vanishing probability. Any claim about how many people attempt a problem
is a claim about setup costs, not about the prize.

\paragraph{Competition is competed away exactly.}
$A^{*}=V_{e}$: in a large pool the effective reward equals the purse at
which a lone researcher would exactly break even. Every researcher
behaves as though facing no competition and a prize of $V_{e}$, and
aggregate effort equals the expected payout. This is complete rent
dissipation, and it is the analogue of the classical free-entry result
with the cost-probability relation derived rather than assumed.

\paragraph{The fixed point is one dimensional.}
Best responses depend on rivals only through the scalar $A_{i}$, which is
why Proposition~\ref{prop:eqfinite} is a one-dimensional root and not a
system. That is the substantive gain from Proposition~\ref{prop:price}.

\section{The committed-width benchmark, and why it fails}
\label{sec:committed}

The first draft studied a different game: attempt widths committed at the
solo-optimal $L=\sigma\sqrt{V/c}$, with only entry chosen. That game is
not the equilibrium, for a reason worth displaying.

\paragraph{The solo width is not a best response.}
Take $V=100$, $c=\sigma=1$, $d=5$, and two entrants both using the solo
width $L=10$, so $p=1/2$ each. Then $A=V(1-p/2)=75$ and the payoff at the
solo width is $75\cdot\tfrac12-25=12.5$. The best response is
$L=\sigma\sqrt{A/c}=\sqrt{75}\approx8.6603$, worth $13.3975$: a gain of
$0.8975$, or $7.2\%$. Competition shortens attempts, and any model that
fixes their length misprices it.

\paragraph{Even that game did not deliver two attempts.}
Suppose widths are committed anyway, so $(p,k=p(1-p)V)$ are exogenous.
Then a set of exactly three entrants, with everyone else out, is a pure
Nash equilibrium whenever $p<2-\sqrt2\approx0.5858$: each incumbent earns
$V\big[1-(1-p)^{3}\big]/3-p(1-p)V=Vp^{3}/3>0$, while a fourth would earn
$Vp^{2}\big(-\tfrac12+p-\tfrac{p^{2}}{4}\big)<0$. Two entrants is never a
pure equilibrium, since the third always gains $Vp^{3}/3>0$ by joining.
The companion paper's exact potential selects $m=3$ for every $p$ up to
about $0.5$. So the first draft's ``two attempts'' was the symmetric mixed
equilibrium and nothing else; the coordinated outcome of the same game is
three. At fixed $n\ge4$ the mixed equilibrium gives
$nq\to2n/(n-1)$ as $p\downarrow0$ --- $2.667$ at $n=4$, $2.041$ at
$n=50$ --- reaching $2$ only in the pool limit. The claim is withdrawn.

\paragraph{It also gets purse design wrong.}
Section~\ref{sec:purse} solves both models exactly. The committed-width
answer is a different constant, and the difference is the whole content of
endogenising the width.

\section{Purse design}
\label{sec:purse}

A funder commits escrowed capital $B$ to identical problems with
break-even purse $V_{e}$, choosing the number $M$ of problems and posting
$V=B/M$ on each; free entry then determines each problem's solve
probability. Write $H(y)=1-e^{-y}$, so $G=H/y$.

\begin{proposition}[Optimal purse]\label{prop:purse}
Under Proposition~\ref{prop:eqlimit}, $V=V_{e}\,y/H(y)$, so
$M=B\,H(y)/(V_{e}y)$ and the expected number of problems solved is
\begin{equation}
\Psi=M\,H(y)=\frac{B}{V_{e}}\cdot\frac{H(y)^{2}}{y}.
\end{equation}
Over the continuous relaxation of $M$, $\Psi$ has a unique positive
maximiser, characterised by
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;e^{y}=1+2y\;}
\end{equation}
at which, exactly,
\begin{equation}
\frac{V^{*}}{V_{e}}=y^{*}+\frac12,
\qquad
\PP(\text{solved})=\frac{2y^{*}}{1+2y^{*}},
\qquad
\Psi^{*}=\frac{B}{V_{e}}\cdot\frac{H(y^{*})^{2}}{y^{*}},
\end{equation}
numerically $y^{*}=1.25643121$, $V^{*}=1.75643121\,V_{e}$,
$\PP(\text{solved})=0.71533186$ and
$\Psi^{*}=0.40726438\,B/V_{e}$ on $M^{*}=0.56933627\,B/V_{e}$
problems. The optimal purse is a fixed multiple of the break-even purse,
independent of $B$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$G(y)=V_{e}/V$ gives $V=V_{e}y/H(y)$ and hence $M$ and $\Psi$.
Maximising $\log\Psi=2\log H-\log y+\text{const}$ gives
$2H'/H=1/y$, and with $H'=e^{-y}$ this is $2ye^{-y}=1-e^{-y}$, i.e.\
$e^{-y}(2y+1)=1$, i.e.\ $e^{y}=1+2y$. That equation has exactly one
positive root, since $e^{y}-1-2y$ is convex with value $0$ and derivative
$-1$ at $y=0$ and tends to $+\infty$. At the root
$H=1-1/(1+2y)=2y/(1+2y)$, so $y/H=(1+2y)/2=y+\tfrac12$, giving
$V^{*}/V_{e}$ and $\PP(\text{solved})=H(y^{*})$.
\end{proof}

For comparison, running the same program through the committed-width
model of Section~\ref{sec:committed}, where $V=V_{\min}\,(y/H)^{2}$,
gives $e^{y}=1+\tfrac32y$, $y^{*}=0.76268856$,
$V^{*}=\big(y^{*}+\tfrac23\big)^{2}V_{\min}=2.04305637\,V_{\min}$ and
$\PP(\text{solved})=0.53358923$. Both constants are confirmed against a
two-million-point brute-force maximisation of the exact objective; the
first draft's $9/4$ and two thirds were an approximation to the second of
these, evaluated at $p=1/3$, where the long-odds approximation it relied
on is not valid. The general form is
$e^{y}=1+(1+1/\text{power})y$, where the power is $1$ when widths adjust
and $2$ when they are committed.

Two caveats. Integer $M$ reintroduces budget-dependent rounding, so
budget independence is a property of the relaxation. And expected count
solved is one objective among many: a funder who cares most about the
hardest problem should not spread at all.

\section{What a purse buys}
\label{sec:buys}

The first draft asserted that purses buy breadth much more cheaply than
depth, and that doubling a purse does almost nothing. The second half is
false and the first is not a general truth.

\paragraph{Near the threshold the purse is powerful.}
For an attempt sized against reward $V$, $p=1-\zeta/\sqrt V$, so
\begin{equation}
\frac{dp}{d\log V}=\frac{1-p}{2},
\qquad
p\;\mapsto\;1-\frac{1-p}{\sqrt2}\ \text{ on doubling }V.
\end{equation}
From $p=0.01$, doubling the purse gives $p=0.29996$; a tenfold purse
gives $0.68694$. The sensitivity is largest exactly where $p$ is
smallest, because small $p$ means $V$ only just exceeds $\zeta^{2}$. In
equilibrium the same steepness appears in \eqref{eq:limit}: near
threshold $y^{*}\approx2(V/V_{e}-1)$, so the expected number of solvers is
proportional to the \emph{excess} of the purse over break-even, and the
optimal purse of Proposition~\ref{prop:purse} sits at $1.76$ times
break-even, in the steep region by construction.

\paragraph{What survives of the depth argument.}
The companion paper's continuation width at discount-plus-hazard
$\rho>0$ is $L=\sigma(2\rho)^{-1/2}\arcosh(1+\rho V/c)$, which grows like
$\log V$ once $\rho V\gg c$, against $\sigma\sqrt{V/c}$ at $\rho=0$. That
is a statement about attempts run far above threshold under positive
discounting. It does not license any claim about the marginal value of a
purse near threshold, and it was misused to make one.

\section{The Clay problems: what can and cannot be said}
\label{sec:clay}

Nothing in this section is empirical, and the first draft was wrong to
present it as though it were. There is one datum with real content and one
theorem with real bite; the rest is arithmetic on stipulated numbers,
which is why the tables are gone.

\paragraph{The datum: the purse is not the prize.}
Perelman's preprints settled the Poincar\'e conjecture in 2002--2003; the
million-dollar purse was awarded in 2010 and declined. A prize refused by
the only person entitled to it is not what was being pursued. The reward
for settling a famous problem is priority, the historical record, and
whatever a career is worth, and it is not denominated in escrow. That the
reward exceeds the notional purse is an observation. \emph{By how much} is
not measured anywhere, by anyone, including here.

\paragraph{The theorem: the surplus is small even when the reward is huge.}
Because $V$ is unobserved, the model cannot predict Millennium effort.
What it can do is say which ratio would settle the question, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:surplus} says it is not the obvious one. However large
the reward, the amount by which it exceeds the walk-away threshold is
$(2-p)/(1-p)$ times the cost of one attempt --- about twice it for long
shots. So the diagnostic for whether a cash purse changes behaviour is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\text{purse}}{2k},
\qquad\text{not}\qquad
\frac{\text{purse}}{V},
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the expected cost of one serious attempt. The first draft
computed the second ratio, found it near one percent, and concluded that
the purses were scoreboards. That inference does not follow, and under the
model it is probably wrong: a purse that is one percent of the level of
the reward can be half of the surplus, because the model's own arithmetic
makes the surplus a multiple of a single attempt's cost rather than a
fraction of the reward.

To see the size of the issue, and purely as arithmetic on round numbers:
if a serious attempt costs \$1M then $\text{purse}/2k=\tfrac12$, and
deleting the purse from the reward roughly halves the equilibrium number
of solvers, from $y^{*}=0.0403$ to $0.0203$; if an attempt costs \$10M the
ratio is $0.05$ and the effect is a five-percent reduction; if an attempt
costs \$200k the purse is decisive, in that without it the reward falls
below the walk-away threshold and nothing is attempted at all. Which of
those is the right order for a Millennium problem is not known to me, and
the model cannot supply it. The honest output is the ratio to go and
measure.

\paragraph{An order of magnitude on rivalry.}
Here the arithmetic belongs to the companion paper's first-solution race,
not to the lottery of Sections~\ref{sec:eq}--\ref{sec:purse}: there a
rival's solution destroys the option, so an attempt faces $\rho=r+h$ and
the share of impatience due to competition is $h/(r+h)$. One solution
since 2000 over an exposure of $6\times26+3=159$ problem-years gives
$\hat h=0.0063$ per problem-year, and the exact Poisson interval for one
event is $[1.6\times10^{-4},\,3.5\times10^{-2}]$. Two corrections both
push the same way: the observed problem-level hazard is the
\emph{aggregate}, including the focal researcher, whose own contribution
must be removed --- with $N$ symmetric attackers the rival hazard is
$(N-1)/N$ of it --- and any positive discount rate enters the
denominator. At $r$ between $3\%$ and $10\%$ the aggregate share runs
$0.06$ to $0.17$, and the rival share with three attackers $0.04$ to
$0.12$; at the interval's upper endpoint and $r=5\%$ the aggregate share
is $0.41$. The defensible claim is only the weak, order-of-magnitude one:
a solution hazard of order $10^{-2}$ per year is not large compared with
any plausible discount rate, so rivalry is unlikely to be the main reason
anyone abandons a Millennium problem. One event supports nothing
stronger, and the interval says so.

\paragraph{What would change it.}
The model localises the whole of the interesting variation in
$\zeta=d\sqrt c/\sigma$. An attempt run at machine cost lowers $c$ and, if
the search is genuinely broader, raises $\sigma$; both lower $\zeta$,
which lowers the break-even purse quadratically, raises $p$, and raises
the expected number of solvers through \eqref{eq:limit}. If that ever
compresses expected time-to-solution from decades to a few years, $h$ rises
by two orders of magnitude and the rivalry share flips from under a tenth
to over four fifths, and only then does the clay-pigeon metaphor of the
title become the right one. That is a forecast, and it is the one part of
the paper that could be checked against something.

\section{What this gets wrong}
\label{sec:wrong}

\paragraph{The lottery is not the race.}
Sections~\ref{sec:eq}--\ref{sec:purse} solve the lottery: everyone pays
their attempt to completion and a winner is drawn among the successes.
In the first-solution race, costs cease when a rival announces, the
rival hazard is a function of calendar time, and the equilibrium is the
companion paper's coupled stopping and forward-distribution system, which
is characterised there and solved nowhere. The two games differ in a way
that matters --- in the race the option is destroyed, in the lottery only
diluted --- so the constants here should not be quoted for a race.

\paragraph{Only symmetric equilibria are proved unique.}
Best responses fall in rivals' success probabilities, so this is a game of
strategic substitutes and asymmetric equilibria are not excluded. The
best-response map is a contraction when
$\zeta\,|A'(p)|/(2A^{3/2})<1$, which holds at every parameter combination
reported here, but that is a check and not a theorem.

\paragraph{Pausing does not by itself remove the threshold.}
The first draft claimed that allowing a pause, as the companion's
intensity extension does with $u=0$, would remove the sharp walk-away
threshold. It would not. With progress frozen while idle, constant
discounting and rival hazard, and no information arriving, waiting
strictly destroys value --- the value function simply decays at rate
$\rho$ --- so the optimal control is bang-bang and the work-or-stop
boundary is unchanged. Resumption after decades requires something to
change while the problem sits: new tools lowering $c$ or raising $\sigma$,
or information arriving from elsewhere. That is a model in which $\zeta$
drifts down over time, which is exactly the mechanism of the previous
section, and it is the honest way to write down a mathematician who comes
back to a problem in twenty years.

\paragraph{Difficulty is common, not private.}
The product survival formula behind \eqref{eq:Ai} and behind the rivalry
share needs independence across researchers. In mathematics the dominant
uncertainty is shared: whether the statement is true, whether the method
can work at all. A rival's visible failure is evidence about one's own
prospects, which turns this into a game with filtering and correlated
states.

\paragraph{Progress is partly public, and credit is not winner-take-all.}
Preprints and talks reveal rival states, which is a joint-state game.
Partial results are published and rewarded, so the all-or-nothing payoff
overstates the waste. Verification also takes years --- the Poincar\'e
purse was paid seven years after the preprints --- so a claimed proof that
later fails restarts the race and the hazard conflates ``someone claims''
with ``someone solves''.

\paragraph{And there are no data.}
No parameter in Section~\ref{sec:clay} is estimated. The cost of a serious
attempt and its success probability would both have to be measured before
any of this touched the world, and measuring them is harder than the
mathematics here.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Racing for a prize.}
\citet{loury1979} and \citet{leewilde1980} set the pattern: a flow of R\&D
spending buys a Poisson hazard of success, and free entry dissipates the
rents. \citet{dasguptastiglitz1980} added industrial structure,
\citet{reinganum1981} the dynamic game, and \citet{fudenberg1983}
preemption and leapfrogging.
\citet{harrisvickers1985,harrisvickers1987} put the race on a state space
with observable positions and studied who gives up. What this paper adds
is a \emph{derived} rather than assumed relation between the cost of an
attempt and its success probability: Proposition~\ref{prop:price} is a
price list, not a specification, and it is what makes the equilibrium of
Section~\ref{sec:eq} a one-dimensional fixed point.

\paragraph{Private progress and preemption.}
\citet{hopenhaynsquintani2011} is the closest neighbour: privately
observed progress, an option to stop, and preemption. \citet{bbm2017}
model the tradeoff between publishing early and polishing under priority
risk. \citet{moscarinisquintani2010} and \citet{krc2005} study
experimentation when rivals' information is a motive to keep going. Those
papers keep the strategic loop closed in the harder allocation --- first
past the post --- and leave the cost-to-probability mapping in reduced
form. This one closes the mapping in the easier allocation.

\paragraph{Contest design.}
\citet{tullock1980} and \citet{baye1996} price rent dissipation;
\citet{taylor1995} and \citet{chegale2003} design research tournaments;
\citet{fullerton1999} auction entry to select contestants;
\citet{halac2017} design contests when the technology must be discovered.
Proposition~\ref{prop:purse} is a design result with an unusually crude
instrument --- how many purses, and how large --- and its answer is a
fixed multiple of the break-even purse.

\paragraph{Entry games and potentials.}
\citet{morgan2012} and \citet{fujiaolu2015} study endogenous entry with
subsequent effort; the companion paper's fixed-attempt lottery and its
exact potential sit in the tradition of \citet{rosenthal1973} and
\citet{monderershapley1996}. Section~\ref{sec:committed} uses that
potential against the first draft of this paper.

\paragraph{Budgeted races.}
The forward--backward architecture of the companion paper is borrowed from
the budgeted Brownian race \citep{cotton2026wavefront}, where one
controller prunes many paths under a path-time budget and is paid the
terminal maximum. There an optimality condition with a shadow price closes
the loop; here a consistency condition between stopping rules and the
reward they share. Its concavity certificates say nothing about
equilibrium, a point the companion makes and this paper inherits.

\section{Closing}

An attempt on a hard problem has a price list, $k(p)=\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)$,
and it is remarkably informative: it makes the cost-probability relation a
theorem rather than a specification, it reduces the competitive
equilibrium to a one-dimensional fixed point in which the effective reward
is competed down exactly to the break-even purse, and it delivers an exact
optimal purse at $1.7564$ times that break-even level. It also says
something uncomfortable about the empirics: the surplus that makes a long
shot worth attempting is about twice the cost of one attempt, whatever the
reward's level, so a purse cannot be dismissed as a rounding error just
because it is one.

The first draft of this paper dismissed the Millennium purses on exactly
that reasoning, and it was wrong to. The honest position is that the
reward is unobserved, the cost of a serious attempt is unmeasured, and the
ratio that decides the question is the purse against twice that cost, not
the purse against the reward. What is left to prove is harder than what
is left to measure: existence and selection for the first-solution race's
coupled equilibrium, whose allocation rule --- the option destroyed rather
than diluted --- is the one the title's metaphor actually describes, and
which nobody has solved.
