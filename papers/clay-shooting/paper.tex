\begin{abstract}
\noindent A prize for an unsolved problem is a clay pigeon: it is thrown
up, several guns fire, only the first hit counts, and every other shell is
spent. This paper closes the parameters of the Brownian stopping race so
that the question ``how much effort does a purse of size $V$ call forth''
has an answer. Running one approach to its optimal abandonment boundary
generates the reduced form of the entry game exactly: an attempt succeeds
with probability $p$, costs $p(1-p)V$ in expectation, and is worth
$Vp^{2}$, so the feasible reduced forms trace a single parabola and no
optimally run attempt ever spends more than a quarter of the prize. With
those costs, free entry is far less crowded than the pool: everybody
attempting is an equilibrium only for $n\le3$ or $p\ge p^{*}(n)\approx
\tfrac12(1+\sqrt{1-4/n}\,)$, and in the long-odds limit the expected
number of entrants falls to exactly two however many capable people
exist. Persistence, by contrast, grows like $\log V$, so purses buy guns
faster than they buy stamina, and the philanthropist's optimal purse is a
fixed multiple of the walk-away threshold, independent of total budget.
Calibrating to the Millennium Prize Problems, the rival hazard supplies
under a tenth of the impatience facing an attempt and the cash purse is
about one percent of the prize that observed effort reveals: those races
are run against cost and the clock, not against each other. Clay shooting
is the right metaphor only once machine-cost attempts compress
time-to-solution from decades to years.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Clay Mathematics Institute committed one million dollars to each of
seven problems in May 2000 \citep{clay2000}. One has been solved. The
purse is famous, and it is almost certainly irrelevant: the effort spent
on those seven problems exceeds seven million dollars by orders of
magnitude, and the one person who earned a purse declined it. Any model
that treats the cash as the incentive gets the wrong answer. A model that
takes the cash as a \emph{measuring rod} can do better, because the same
arithmetic that fails to explain the effort tells us what the effort
reveals.

The machinery is a companion paper's \citep{cotton2026races}. There, $n$
researchers choose whether to attempt a prize problem; an attempt is a
privately observed diffusion that wins on first passage to a target,
costs a flow rate while it runs, and may be irreversibly abandoned. That
paper supplies two ingredients and stops: a one-period entry game with an
exact potential, and a closed-form abandonment boundary for a driftless
attempt facing a constant rival hazard. It leaves $(p,k)$ --- the success
probability and expected cost of an attempt --- as free parameters of the
entry game, which is exactly what makes the entry game unfalsifiable.

This paper closes that loop and then spends the closure. Three results.

\emph{The reduced form is a parabola.} An attempt run to its own optimal
boundary is not free to have any $(p,k)$. In the driftless undiscounted
case, $k=p(1-p)V$ and the attempt is worth $Vp^{2}$, identically
(Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}). The entry game therefore has one free
parameter, not two, and the companion's profitability assumption $k<Vp$
holds automatically. A corollary that a contest designer should know:
expected spend per attempt is at most $V/4$.

\emph{Free entry is thin.} Substituting those costs, everybody attempting
is an equilibrium for $n\le3$ at any $p$, and for $n\ge4$ only when $p$
exceeds a threshold $p^{*}(n)$ that we compute in closed form and that
rises to $1$ like $1-1/n$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:universal}). In a large
pool the interior equilibrium has expected entrants
$N^{*}(p)$ that is increasing in $p$ with infimum exactly $2$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:volume}). Long odds do not summon a crowd. They
summon two.

\emph{Depth is dearer than breadth.} The continuation width bought by a
purse grows like $\sqrt V$ undiscounted and like $\log V$ once
discounting or rivalry bites, while entry responds at the margin of a
linear comparison. A philanthropist splitting committed capital across
identical problems should therefore set each purse at a fixed small
multiple of the minimum viable purse --- about $2.25$ times it in the
long-odds closed form, $2.04$ times it in the exact numerical solve ---
and put every remaining dollar into more problems
(Proposition~\ref{prop:purse}).

Section~\ref{sec:clay} spends the closure on the Millennium problems.
Three inversions: the rival hazard supplies between $5\%$ and $16\%$ of
the effective impatience facing an attempt, so those races are run
against cost and the clock; the purse is $0.4\%$ to $3.3\%$ of the prize
that observed effort reveals; and the cash alone funds about three years
of one person's diffusive travel. The clay-pigeon metaphor is therefore
wrong for the Millennium problems as they have actually been attempted,
and becomes right precisely when the cost of an attempt falls to machine
prices, which raises the solution hazard and hands abandonment over to
rivalry for the first time.

\paragraph{What is and is not claimed.}
Propositions~\ref{prop:reduction}--\ref{prop:purse} are proved.
Section~\ref{sec:clay} is a calibration under stated dials, not an
estimation: the per-attempt success probability and the cost of a serious
assault are stipulated, and the solution hazard is a Poisson rate
estimated from a single event. Section~\ref{sec:wrong} lists the
modelling assumptions that mathematics violates, one of which ---
irreversibility of abandonment --- it violates flatly.

\section{The race that generates the entry game}
\label{sec:reduction}

Fix one researcher and one approach. While working, the researcher
observes $dX_{t}=\sigma\,dW_{t}$ with $\sigma>0$, starting at $x_{0}<b$;
first passage to $b$ is a solution worth $V$; working costs $c>0$ per
unit time; abandonment is irreversible. Write
\begin{equation}
d=b-x_{0}
\end{equation}
for the distance still to travel. Let $\rho=r+h$ collect the discount
rate and any constant rival solution hazard. The companion paper's
boundary result \citep[Prop.~3]{cotton2026races} is that the optimal
policy continues on an interval $(a,b)$ of width
\begin{equation}
L(V)=b-a=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\,
 \arcosh\!\left(1+\dfrac{\rho V}{c}\right), & \rho>0,\\[10pt]
\sigma\sqrt{V/c}, & \rho=0,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:width}
\end{equation}
and abandons at or below $a$. Entry is worthwhile at all only if
$L(V)>d$.

The reduced form of an \emph{attempt} is the pair
\begin{equation}
p=\PP\big(\text{solve before abandoning}\big),
\qquad
k=c\,\E\big[\text{time spent}\big],
\label{eq:reducedform}
\end{equation}
both computed without discounting, since these are the objects the
one-period entry game consumes. The following identity is the point of
this section.

\begin{proposition}[Exact reduction]\label{prop:reduction}
Let $\rho=0$ and define the \emph{hardness index}
\begin{equation}
\zeta=\frac{d\sqrt c}{\sigma},
\qquad\text{so that}\qquad
\zeta^{2}=c\cdot\frac{d^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.
\label{eq:zeta}
\end{equation}
If $V\le\zeta^{2}$ the optimal policy abandons at once and the attempt is
worthless. If $V>\zeta^{2}$, then
\begin{equation}
p=1-\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt V},
\qquad
k=p(1-p)\,V,
\qquad
Vp-k=Vp^{2}=\big(\sqrt V-\zeta\big)^{2},
\label{eq:reduction}
\end{equation}
and $Vp^{2}$ is the value of the attempt.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $\rho=0$ the boundary is $a=b-L$, $L=\sigma\sqrt{V/c}$, and
$x_{0}-a=L-d$. For driftless Brownian motion on the bounded interval
$(a,b)$, optional stopping applied to $X$ and to $X_{t}^{2}-\sigma^{2}t$
at $\tau=\tau_{a}\wedge\tau_{b}$ --- legitimate because $X$ is bounded on
the interval and $\E[\tau]<\infty$ --- gives the two gambler's-ruin
identities
\begin{equation}
p=\frac{x_{0}-a}{b-a}=1-\frac{d}{L},
\qquad
\E[\tau]=\frac{(x_{0}-a)(b-x_{0})}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{d\,(L-d)}{\sigma^{2}}.
\label{eq:ruin}
\end{equation}
Since $L=\sigma\sqrt{V/c}$ we have $d/L=\zeta/\sqrt V$, which is the
first claim, and $\sigma^{2}=cL^{2}/V$, so
\[
k=c\,\E[\tau]=\frac{c\,d(L-d)}{\sigma^{2}}
 =V\cdot\frac{d}{L}\left(1-\frac{d}{L}\right)=V(1-p)p .
\]
Hence $Vp-k=Vp^{2}$, and $Vp^{2}=V(1-\zeta/\sqrt V)^{2}
=(\sqrt V-\zeta)^{2}$. That this expected net payoff equals the value
follows because the policy in question is the optimal one; directly, the
companion's closed form gives
$v(x_{0})=c(x_{0}-a)^{2}/\sigma^{2}=Vp^{2}$. Finally $L>d$ is equivalent
to $V>\zeta^{2}$, and at or below that purse the value is zero.
\end{proof}

Three readings of \eqref{eq:reduction}.

\paragraph{The minimum viable purse is a travel time.}
$V_{\min}=\zeta^{2}=c\,d^{2}/\sigma^{2}$, and $d^{2}/\sigma^{2}$ is
exactly the diffusive time for the state to cover the remaining distance
$d$. So the walk-away threshold is the cost rate times the diffusive
traverse: a purse must cover an undirected crossing of the gap before
anyone will start, and it must do so out of its own pocket, because at
$\rho=0$ nothing else discounts the effort.

\paragraph{The feasible reduced forms are one curve.}
$(V,c,\sigma,d)$ enter $p$, $k/V$ and $v/V$ only through the single ratio
$\zeta/\sqrt V=1-p$.

\begin{corollary}[The entry game has one parameter]\label{cor:parabola}
As $(V,c,\sigma,d)$ range over the positives with $V>\zeta^{2}$, the
attainable pairs $(p,k/V)$ are exactly the arc
$\{(p,p(1-p)):p\in(0,1)\}$. Consequently $k<Vp$ always holds strictly,
and $k\le V/4$, with equality only at $p=1/2$.
\end{corollary}

The bound is worth stating for its own sake: an attempt run to its own
optimal boundary never spends, in expectation, more than a quarter of the
prize, and spends that much only when it is a coin flip. Cheap long shots
and near-certainties both spend little; the expensive attempts are the
genuinely uncertain ones.

\paragraph{Discounting breaks the identity, not the method.}
For $\rho>0$ the width \eqref{eq:width} is no longer
$\sigma\sqrt{V/c}$, so $k=c\,d(L-d)/\sigma^{2}$ from \eqref{eq:ruin}
still holds but the clean relation $k=p(1-p)V$ does not. The honest
statement is that the exact reduction belongs to the undiscounted
benchmark, which is also the only case in which feeding undiscounted
$(p,k)$ into an undiscounted entry game is internally consistent. With
$\rho>0$ both sides must be discounted together; we do not pursue that
here, and every entry result below is a result about the $\rho=0$
reduced form.

\paragraph{A verification.}
A committed, seeded Euler simulation of the attempt --- boundary location
and increment law as its only inputs --- reproduces
\eqref{eq:reduction}. At $V=100$, $c=\sigma=1$, $d=4$, so $\zeta=4$,
$L=10$ and $p=0.6$, fifty thousand paths at $dt=2\times10^{-3}$ give
$\hat p=0.6051$ with standard error $0.0022$ and
$\hat k=24.05$ with standard error $0.092$, against $p=0.6$ and
$k=p(1-p)V=24$. The small upward bias in $\hat p$ is Euler overshoot of
the upper boundary, and it shrinks with $dt$.

\section{How many guns}
\label{sec:guns}

Take $n$ symmetric researchers facing one problem, each with the reduced
form of Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} at common $p$, each entering
with probability $q$. Ties among successes are broken uniformly and only
one purse is paid. The companion's symmetric entry condition
\citep[\S2]{cotton2026races} is that the gross return to entering against
common probability $q$,
\begin{equation}
R(q)=\frac{V\big[1-(1-pq)^{n}\big]}{nq},
\label{eq:R}
\end{equation}
be compared with $k$: universal entry $q=1$ is an equilibrium iff
$k\le R(1)$, and an interior equilibrium solves $R(q)=k$.

\subsection{When everybody attempts}

\begin{proposition}[Universal entry]\label{prop:universal}
Substitute $k=p(1-p)V$ and write $w=1-p$. Then $q=1$ is a symmetric
equilibrium if and only if
\begin{equation}
\varphi_{n}(w):=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}w^{j}-nw\;\ge\;0 .
\label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
For $n=2$ and $n=3$ this holds at every $p$, since
$\varphi_{2}(w)=p$ and $\varphi_{3}(w)=p^{2}$. For $n\ge4$ there is a
unique $w_{n}\in(0,1)$ with $\varphi_{n}(w_{n})=0$, and $q=1$ is an
equilibrium exactly when
\begin{equation}
p\;\ge\;p^{*}(n):=1-w_{n}.
\end{equation}
Moreover $w_{n}$ solves $n\,w(1-w)=1-w^{n}$, so dropping the term $w^{n}$
gives the closed form
\begin{equation}
\hat p^{*}(n)=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4/n}}{2}
 \;=\;1-\frac1n-\frac1{n^{2}}+O(n^{-3}),
\label{eq:pstar}
\end{equation}
whose discriminant vanishes precisely at $n=4$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Universal entry requires
$p(1-p)V\le V\big[1-(1-p)^{n}\big]/n$, that is
$n\,p\,w\le 1-w^{n}=(1-w)\sum_{j<n}w^{j}=p\sum_{j<n}w^{j}$. Dividing by
$p>0$ gives \eqref{eq:phi}. Now
$\varphi_{n}'(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}jw^{j-1}-n$ is nondecreasing on
$[0,1]$, so $\varphi_{n}$ is convex there, with
$\varphi_{n}(0)=1>0$, $\varphi_{n}(1)=0$, $\varphi_{n}'(0)=1-n<0$ and
$\varphi_{n}'(1)=n(n-3)/2$.

If $n\le3$ then $\varphi_{n}'(1)\le0$, and convexity forces
$\varphi_{n}'\le0$ throughout $[0,1]$; a nonincreasing function with
$\varphi_{n}(1)=0$ is nonnegative on $[0,1]$. If $n\ge4$ then
$\varphi_{n}'(1)>0$, so $\varphi_{n}<0$ just left of $1$; combined with
$\varphi_{n}(0)>0$ and convexity, $\varphi_{n}$ has exactly one zero
$w_{n}$ in $(0,1)$, is positive on $[0,w_{n})$ and negative on
$(w_{n},1)$. Hence \eqref{eq:phi} holds iff $w\le w_{n}$, i.e.\
$p\ge1-w_{n}$. The stated equation for $w_{n}$ is $\varphi_{n}=0$
multiplied by $1-w$, and \eqref{eq:pstar} is the smaller root of
$nw(1-w)=1$ subtracted from one.
\end{proof}

The neglected term is $w_{n}^{n}\approx n^{-n}$, so \eqref{eq:pstar} is
not merely asymptotic but numerically exact very quickly. In the
committed table it errs by $8.6\times10^{-2}$ at $n=4$,
$7.1\times10^{-4}$ at $n=5$, $3.7\times10^{-8}$ at $n=8$, and by less
than machine precision from $n=20$ on.

\subsection{The volume of a free-entry race}

\begin{proposition}[Free-entry volume]\label{prop:volume}
Let $n\to\infty$ with expected entrants $N=nq$ held fixed, so $q\to0$.
The interior symmetric equilibrium condition $R(q)=k$ with
$k=p(1-p)V$ becomes, in the limit,
\begin{equation}
1-e^{-y}=y(1-p),\qquad y=pN .
\label{eq:volumeeq}
\end{equation}
Because $G(y)=(1-e^{-y})/y$ decreases strictly from $1$ to $0$ on
$(0,\infty)$, \eqref{eq:volumeeq} has a unique root $y^{*}(p)>0$ for
every $p\in(0,1)$, and
\begin{equation}
N^{*}(p)=\frac{y^{*}(p)}{p},
\qquad
\PP(\text{solved})=1-e^{-y^{*}(p)},
\qquad
\E[\text{solvers}]=y^{*}(p).
\end{equation}
$N^{*}$ is strictly increasing in $p$, with
\begin{equation}
N^{*}(p)=2+\tfrac43p+O(p^{2})\quad (p\downarrow0),
\qquad
N^{*}(p)\to\infty\quad(p\uparrow1),
\end{equation}
so $\inf_{p}N^{*}=2$. Aggregate expected expenditure equals the expected
purse payout: $N^{*}k=V(1-e^{-y^{*}})$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$R(q)=k$ reads $V[1-(1-pq)^{n}]=nqk=Np(1-p)V$, and
$(1-pN/n)^{n}\to e^{-pN}$, giving \eqref{eq:volumeeq}. $G$ is strictly
decreasing with $G(0^{+})=1$, $G(\infty)=0$, so the root is unique and
$y^{*}$ is strictly increasing in $p$. Parametrise by $y$, with
$p=1-G(y)$ and $N=y/(1-G(y))$. Then
\[
\frac{dN}{dy}=\frac{(1-G(y))+yG'(y)}{(1-G(y))^{2}},
\qquad
(1-G)+yG'=\frac{y-2+e^{-y}(2+y)}{y}=:\frac{\psi(y)}{y},
\]
and $\psi(0)=0$ with
$\psi'(y)=1-e^{-y}(1+y)>0$ for $y>0$ because $e^{y}>1+y$. So
$\psi>0$, $N$ is strictly increasing along the parametrisation, and hence
$N^{*}$ is strictly increasing in $p$. Expanding
$G(y)=1-y/2+y^{2}/6-\dots$ in \eqref{eq:volumeeq} gives
$y^{*}=2p+\tfrac43p^{2}+O(p^{3})$ and therefore
$N^{*}=2+\tfrac43p+O(p^{2})$. As $p\uparrow1$, $G(y^{*})\to0$ forces
$y^{*}\to\infty$ and $N^{*}\ge y^{*}\to\infty$. Finally
$N^{*}k=N^{*}p(1-p)V=y^{*}(1-p)V=(1-e^{-y^{*}})V$ by
\eqref{eq:volumeeq}, which is the expected payout.
\end{proof}

that the limit is a good description of a merely large pool. At $p=0.99$
and $n=50$ it returns $N=50$: universal entry, consistent with
$p^{*}(50)=0.9796$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:universal}, while at
$n=1000$ the same $p$ gives an interior $N=101$, consistent with
$p^{*}(1000)=0.99900$.

Proposition~\ref{prop:volume} is the paper's most compressible finding.
\emph{At long odds, free entry produces two attempts in expectation, no
matter how many people could have attempted.} The two-researcher case of
the companion paper reaches the same number from the other direction: its
symmetric mixed solution is $q=(2/p)(1-k/(Vp))=2$, which truncates to
$q=1$, so both enter and $N=2$ exactly. The infimum is attained in the
limit, not at any $p$, and rent dissipation is complete at every $p$:
the pool spends the purse.

\section{Breadth and depth}
\label{sec:breadth}

Money can buy two things: more attempts, or longer ones. They have very
different prices.

\paragraph{Depth is logarithmic.}
Persistence is the continuation width \eqref{eq:width}. Since
$\arcosh(1+z)\sim\log(2z)$ for large $z$,
\begin{equation}
L(V)\;\approx\;\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\,
 \log\!\frac{2\rho V}{c}
\qquad\text{for }\rho V\gg c,
\end{equation}
and only $\sigma\sqrt{V/c}$ at $\rho=0$. In units where
$c=\sigma=1$ and $\rho=0.05$, multiplying the purse by $100$ multiplies
the continuation width by $7.87$; by $10{,}000$, only by $21.9$. Under
$\rho=0$ the same multiples give $10$ and $100$. Either way the marginal
dollar buys steeply less stamina, and the more competition there is (the
larger $\rho$), the flatter the curve.

\paragraph{Breadth is cheaper per dollar.}
Entry responds through $p(V)=1-\zeta/\sqrt V$ and then through
$N^{*}(p)$, but the decisive asymmetry is that a second problem is bought
outright at price $V$, while a second unit of persistence on one problem
is bought at an exponentially rising price. That comparison has a clean
consequence.

\begin{proposition}[Optimal purse splitting]\label{prop:purse}
A funder commits capital $B$, fully escrowed, to identical problems of
hardness $\zeta$, choosing the number $M$ of problems and posting
$V=B/M$ on each; free entry then determines each problem's solve
probability by Proposition~\ref{prop:volume}. Maximising the expected
number solved,
\begin{equation}
\Psi(M)=M\Big(1-e^{-y^{*}(p(B/M))}\Big),
\qquad p(V)=\Big(1-\zeta/\sqrt V\Big)_{+},
\end{equation}
in the long-odds regime where $1-e^{-y^{*}(p)}=2p+O(p^{2})$ gives
\begin{equation}
M^{*}=\frac{4B}{9\zeta^{2}},
\qquad
V^{*}=\frac94\,\zeta^{2}=\frac94V_{\min},
\qquad
\Psi(M^{*})=\frac{8B}{27\zeta^{2}},
\end{equation}
so the optimal purse is a fixed multiple of the minimum viable purse,
independent of $B$, and two thirds of funded problems are solved.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $p(B/M)=1-\zeta\sqrt{M/B}$ and the long-odds approximation,
$\Psi(M)\approx2M-2\zeta M^{3/2}/\sqrt B$. Differentiating,
$2-3\zeta M^{1/2}/\sqrt B=0$ gives $\sqrt{M^{*}}=2\sqrt B/(3\zeta)$,
hence $M^{*}=4B/(9\zeta^{2})$ and $V^{*}=B/M^{*}=\tfrac94\zeta^{2}$.
Substituting, $\Psi=8B/(9\zeta^{2})-16B/(27\zeta^{2})=8B/(27\zeta^{2})$,
and $p(V^{*})=1-\zeta/(\tfrac32\zeta)=\tfrac13$, so the per-problem solve
probability is $2p=\tfrac23$.
\end{proof}

The approximation is doing real work and should be checked, since
$p=1/3$ is not long odds. Solving the exact problem numerically at
$B=100\zeta^{2}$ over integer $M$ returns $M=49$, a purse of
$2.04\,V_{\min}$, $p=0.300$, and $26.1$ problems solved in expectation
against the closed form's $M=44.4$, $2.25\,V_{\min}$ and $29.6$. The
purse multiple is close; the harvest is overstated by $13\%$, because
$2p=0.600$ overstates the true solve probability $1-e^{-y^{*}}=0.533$ at
$p=0.3$. The qualitative conclusion --- set the purse just above the
walk-away threshold, spend the rest on more problems --- survives, and the
mechanism behind it is worth naming: a purse below $V_{\min}$ buys
nothing at all, a purse far above it buys logarithmically little extra
depth, and the free-entry response caps the crowd at two anyway.

Two caveats. Expected count solved is one objective among many; a funder
who cares more about the hardest problem should not spread. And $B$ is
committed capital, not expected outlay --- the escrow is what makes $M$
the choice variable.

\section{Clay}
\label{sec:clay}

Seven problems were posted in May 2000 at one million dollars each
\citep{clay2000}. Perelman's preprints settled the Poincar\'e conjecture
in 2002--2003; the prize was awarded in 2010 and declined. Six remain
open as of September 2026. Everything below reads that record through
Sections~\ref{sec:reduction}--\ref{sec:breadth} with dials stated
explicitly. None of it is an estimate of a structural parameter.

\subsection{Is the prize a clay pigeon?}

The companion paper's mechanism is that a rival's solution destroys the
option, so the rival hazard $h$ acts as extra discounting: an attempt
faces $\rho=r+h$. The natural diagnostic is therefore the share of
effective impatience that competition supplies,
\begin{equation}
\text{rivalry share}=\frac{h}{r+h}.
\label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
One solution in $7\times26=182$ problem-years gives
$\hat h=0.0055$ per problem-year. Then

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rccc}
\toprule
$r$ & Millennium ($\hat h=0.0055$) & warm ($h=0.1$) & hot ($h=1/3$)\\
\midrule
0.03 & 0.155 & 0.769 & 0.917\\
0.05 & 0.099 & 0.667 & 0.870\\
0.10 & 0.052 & 0.500 & 0.769\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At any plausible discount rate, rivalry supplies well under a sixth of
the pressure to quit a Millennium problem. The purse is not a clay pigeon
being shot at; it is a target sitting in a field, and the reason people
walk away from it is that the shells cost money and the years pass.

One event is one event, and the sampling uncertainty is enormous: the
exact Poisson interval for $\hat h$ is
$[1.4\times10^{-4},\,3.1\times10^{-2}]$ per problem-year. The
conclusion survives it in a weak but useful sense --- even at the upper
endpoint, the rivalry share at $r=0.05$ is $0.38$, still under a half ---
and fails to survive it in the sense that the interval cannot rule out
rivalry mattering an order of magnitude more than the point estimate
says.

\subsection{What the effort reveals about the prize}

Invert $k=p(1-p)V$: an agent who runs a serious attempt costing $k$ with
per-attempt success probability $p$ reveals
$V=k/\big(p(1-p)\big)$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
\toprule
attempt cost $k$ & $p=0.005$ & $p=0.01$ & $p=0.02$\\
\midrule
\$0.6M & \$121M & \$61M & \$31M\\
\$1.0M & \$201M & \$101M & \$51M\\
\$1.2M & \$241M & \$121M & \$61M\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Read the dials as: three focused years of a senior mathematician at
\$200k--\$400k per year all-in, and a per-attempt success probability
between one in two hundred and one in fifty. The implied prize is \$31M
to \$241M, so the one-million-dollar purse is between $0.4\%$ and
$3.3\%$ of it --- one percent in the central cell. The purse is a
scoreboard. Equivalently, the model says the cash would have to rise by a
factor of fifty to two hundred before it became the marginal
consideration for anyone already inclined to attempt.

A second, cruder inversion says the same thing from the cost side. The
minimum viable purse is $V_{\min}=c\,d^{2}/\sigma^{2}$, the cost rate
times the diffusive traverse of the remaining distance. At
$c=\$300$k per year, a \$1M purse funds a traverse of $3.3$ years and no
more. The Millennium problems have absorbed careers. Whatever is paying
for those careers, it is not the escrow.

\subsection{Two guns, and a long tail the model forbids}

Proposition~\ref{prop:volume} predicts, at $p$ of a percent, an expected
$N^{*}\approx2.01$ serious attempts per problem regardless of how many
mathematicians could mount one, with a solve probability of about $2p$:
free entry buys exactly one extra attempt over the lone-researcher
benchmark. The small number of publicly identified long-run attackers per
Millennium problem is consistent with that. The very large number of
amateur attempts is not, and the mismatch is informative rather than
embarrassing: under these dials such attempts have $D_{i}<0$ in the
companion's entry condition, so they cannot be prize-seeking. They are
either heterogeneity the symmetric model suppresses --- a few people with
much higher private $p_{i}$ or much lower $k_{i}$ --- or consumption, in
which case the relevant $V$ is not a prize at all. Either way the
homogeneous free-entry count describes the serious tail of the
distribution and nothing else.

\subsection{When clay shooting starts}
The interesting parameter is not the purse. It is $\zeta=d\sqrt c/\sigma$
and, through it, the hazard. An attempt run at machine cost lowers $c$
and, if the search is genuinely broader, raises $\sigma$; both push
$\zeta$ down, which raises $p=1-\zeta/\sqrt V$, lowers
$V_{\min}=\zeta^{2}$, and raises $N^{*}$. The composition of those
effects is a rise in the solution hazard, and that is the switch.

The model's prediction is sharp about what changes and what does not.
Doubling the purse does almost nothing, by Section~\ref{sec:breadth}.
Cutting $\zeta$ in half quarters the minimum viable purse and moves $p$
from, say, $0.01$ to $0.505$ at fixed $V$, which by
Proposition~\ref{prop:volume} takes $N^{*}$ from $2.01$ to $3.2$ and the
solve probability from $0.02$ to $0.80$. Once expected time-to-solution
falls from decades to a few years, $h$ moves from $0.005$ to order
$0.3$, and the rivalry share in \eqref{eq:share} moves from $0.10$ to
$0.87$. Only then is the purse a clay pigeon, and only then does the
companion paper's coupled hazard equilibrium, rather than its
single-agent boundary, do the explaining. The observable signature would
be a burst of entry followed by rapid abandonment by all but the leaders
--- a pattern the human record on these problems does not contain,
because in the human record nobody is racing anybody.

\section{What this gets wrong about mathematics}
\label{sec:wrong}

The list matters more than usual here, because the calibration in
Section~\ref{sec:clay} is only as good as the modelling choices behind
it, and one of them is simply false.

\paragraph{Abandonment is not irreversible.}
Mathematicians shelve problems and come back to them decades later. The
baseline's only control is an irreversible kill, which is the right
primitive for a metered compute job and the wrong one for a person. The
right home for the human version is the intensity extension of the
companion paper, in which $u=0$ is a pause rather than a death; that
changes the boundary and, in particular, removes the sharp walk-away
threshold that $V_{\min}$ represents. Every statement here that leans on
$V_{\min}$ --- including Proposition~\ref{prop:purse} --- should be read
as a statement about the committed-attempt reading.

\paragraph{Difficulty is common, not private.}
The product survival formula behind \eqref{eq:share} needs independence
across researchers. In mathematics the dominant uncertainty is shared:
whether the statement is true, whether the method can work at all. A
rival's visible failure is evidence about your own prospects, which turns
the game into one with filtering and correlated states, and there the
rival hazard is no longer a deterministic function of calendar time.

\paragraph{Progress is partly public.}
Preprints, seminars and conference talks reveal rival states. That is a
joint-state game, and the private-diffusion structure is a
simplification chosen for tractability, not realism.

\paragraph{Winner-take-all is an approximation.}
Partial results are published, cited and rewarded; a lemma that survives
into the eventual proof pays something. The all-or-nothing first-passage
payoff overstates the waste and therefore, through
Corollary~\ref{cor:parabola}, understates the effort a given prize should
call forth.

\paragraph{Verification takes years.}
The Poincar\'e purse was paid seven years after the preprints. A claimed
proof that later fails restarts the race, so the announcement is not an
absorbing event, and the hazard in \eqref{eq:share} conflates ``someone
claims'' with ``someone solves''.

\paragraph{The dials are stipulated.}
$p$, $k$ and $r$ in Section~\ref{sec:clay} are assumptions. The
inversions are arithmetic on them, and their only real content is
robustness: to overturn the conclusion that the purse is a scoreboard,
one needs $p(1-p)V$ near \$1M, which at $k=\$1$M requires $p$ near $1/2$.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Racing for a prize.}
\citet{loury1979} and \citet{leewilde1980} set the pattern: a flow of R\&D
spending buys a Poisson hazard of success, and free entry dissipates the
rents. \citet{dasguptastiglitz1980} added industrial structure,
\citet{reinganum1981} the dynamic game, and
\citet{fudenberg1983} preemption and leapfrogging.
\citet{harrisvickers1985,harrisvickers1987} put the race on a state space
with observable positions and studied who gives up. What we add to that
tradition is a \emph{derived} rather than assumed relation between the
cost of an attempt and its success probability: in
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} the pair is not chosen, it falls out of
running a diffusion to its own optimal boundary, and $k=p(1-p)V$ is the
constraint the Poisson-hazard formulations leave free.

\paragraph{Private progress and preemption.}
\citet{hopenhaynsquintani2011} is the closest neighbour: privately
observed progress, an option to stop, and preemption. \citet{bbm2017}
model the researcher's own tradeoff between publishing early and
polishing under priority risk. \citet{moscarinisquintani2010} and
\citet{krc2005} study experimentation when rivals' information is a
motive to keep going. All of those keep the strategic loop closed and
leave the cost-to-probability mapping in reduced form; we do the
opposite, taking the loop as given by the companion paper and closing the
mapping instead.

\paragraph{Contest design.}
\citet{tullock1980} and \citet{baye1996} price rent dissipation;
\citet{taylor1995} and \citet{chegale2003} design research tournaments;
\citet{fullerton1999} auction entry to select contestants;
\citet{halac2017} design contests when the technology must be discovered.
Proposition~\ref{prop:purse} is a design result of the same kind with an
unusually crude instrument: the designer chooses only how many purses to
post and how large, and the answer is a fixed multiple of the walk-away
threshold.

\paragraph{Entry games and potentials.}
\citet{morgan2012} and \citet{fujiaolu2015} study endogenous entry with
subsequent effort; the companion paper's fixed-attempt lottery and its
exact potential sit in the tradition of \citet{rosenthal1973} and
\citet{monderershapley1996}. Corollary~\ref{cor:parabola} says which
points of that game's parameter space a Brownian attempt can actually
occupy.

\paragraph{Budgeted races.}
The forward--backward architecture of the companion paper is borrowed from
the budgeted Brownian race \citep{cotton2026wavefront}, where a single
controller prunes many paths under a path-time budget and is paid the
terminal maximum. The two differ in what closes the loop: there an
optimality condition with a shadow price, here a consistency condition
between each researcher's stopping rule and the hazard the others
generate. Its concavity certificates therefore say nothing about
equilibrium here, a point the companion paper makes and this one inherits.

\section{Closing}

The arithmetic of this paper is elementary and its conclusion is
deflationary. Closing the parameters of a Brownian prize race pins the
cost of an attempt to its success probability, and that single constraint
is enough to show that free entry is thin, that persistence is expensive,
and that a purse should be set just above the threshold at which anyone
will start. Applied to the most famous prizes in mathematics, the same
arithmetic says that those races are not races: the hazard a rival
imposes is a tenth of the impatience an attempt faces, and the cash is a
hundredth of the prize the effort reveals.

That makes the interesting question a forecast rather than a measurement.
The model localises the whole of the change in one index,
$\zeta=d\sqrt c/\sigma$, and says that when the cost of an attempt falls
to machine prices the rivalry share flips from a tenth to nine tenths.
At that point the strategic fixed point of the companion paper stops
being an elegant characterisation and starts being the object one has to
compute. Three things would have to be settled first: existence and
selection for the coupled equilibrium, a convergence guarantee for its
iteration, and an honest treatment of common difficulty, which is the
assumption mathematics violates most and which no calendar-time hazard
can absorb.
