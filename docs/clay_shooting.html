Clay Shooting

Equilibrium effort solving outstanding problems.

Peter Cotton · working draft, second revision, September 2026 · tex · numerics · output

Abstract

A Brownian attempt on a prize problem has a price list. Running one to an abandonment floor buys success probability \(p\) at expected cost \(k(p)=\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)\), where \(\zeta^{2}\) is the cost of a diffusive traverse of the remaining distance; the identity holds for every floor, so an attempt's cost and success rate pin \(\zeta^{2}\) with no appeal to optimality, discounting, or the size of the prize. Sizing the attempt against an effective reward \(A\) gives \(p=1-\zeta/\sqrt A\), \(k=p(1-p)A\) and value \((\sqrt A-\zeta)^{2}\); in a lottery for one prize \(A\) is the shared reward, not the purse, which is where a fixed-attempt entry game misprices competition. Closing that loop, the symmetric equilibrium attempt size is the unique root of \(p=1-\zeta/\sqrt{A(p)}\), and in a large pool it collapses to \(G(y^{*})=V_{e}/V\), where \(G(y)=(1-e^{-y})/y\), \(y^{*}\) is the expected number of solvers and \(V_{e}\) the purse at which a lone researcher breaks even: the effective reward is competed down to \(V_{e}\) exactly, rents dissipate completely, and the number of entrants is not pinned at all. A funder splitting escrowed capital should post purses of \((y^{*}+\tfrac12)V_{e}=1.7564\,V_{e}\) where \(e^{y}=1+2y\), solving 71.5% of funded problems. The reward can exceed the walk-away threshold by only \((2-p)/(1-p)\) times the cost of one attempt, so whether a cash purse matters is governed by its ratio to twice that cost and not to the prize — which is why nothing here identifies the Millennium purses' role, and why this draft withdraws the claim that they are scoreboards.

1. Introduction

The Clay Mathematics Institute committed one million dollars to each of seven problems in May 2000; one has been solved, and the person entitled to the purse refused it. That refusal is the most informative single fact anyone has about the reward structure of famous mathematics, and what it says is that the reward is not denominated in the purse. It does not say by how much, and neither does this paper.

What this paper does is price the attempt. A companion note sets up a prize race: researchers choose whether to attempt a problem, an attempt is a privately observed diffusion that wins on first passage to a target, costs a flow rate while it runs, and may be irreversibly abandoned. It leaves the success probability \(p\) and expected cost \(k\) of an attempt as free parameters of its entry game. Here they are derived, and the derivation turns out to constrain the game far more than the first draft of this paper recognised.

Three results.

First, a price list (Section 2). For an attempt run between a target at distance \(d\) and an abandonment floor, \(k(p)=\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)\) with \(\zeta^{2}=c\,d^{2}/\sigma^{2}\), the cost rate times the diffusive traverse of the distance still to go. This holds for every floor, optimal or not, at any discount rate, so an observed \((k,p)\) pair identifies \(\zeta^{2}\) outright. Sizing the attempt optimally against an effective reward \(A\) gives \(p=1-\zeta/\sqrt A\), \(k=p(1-p)A\) and value \((\sqrt A-\zeta)^{2}\). A corollary that matters later: the reward can exceed the walk-away threshold \(\zeta^{2}\) by only \((2-p)/(1-p)\) times the cost of a single attempt.

Second, an equilibrium in attempt sizes (Section 3). In a lottery for one prize the reward conditional on success is shared, \(A_{i}=V\,\mathbb{E}[(1+K_{-i})^{-1}]<V\), so an attempt sized against the full purse is too long. The symmetric equilibrium is the unique root of \(p=1-\zeta/\sqrt{A(p)}\), and in a large pool it becomes \(G(y^{*})=V_{e}/V\) with \(G(y)=(1-e^{-y})/y\), \(y^{*}\) the expected number of solvers and \(V_{e}\) the purse at which a lone researcher exactly breaks even. The effective reward is competed down to \(V_{e}\) exactly and rents dissipate completely. What is pinned is the number of solutions; the number of entrants diverges as the setup cost vanishes.

Third, an exact purse design (Section 5). A funder splitting escrowed capital \(B\) across identical problems maximises the expected number solved at \(e^{y}=1+2y\), giving \(V^{*}/V_{e}=y^{*}+\tfrac12=1.75643121\) and a solve probability of \(0.71533186\), independent of \(B\). The budget chooses the number of problems; the purse is a fixed multiple of the break-even purse.

Corrections to the first draft

A referee found four substantive errors, and correcting them changes the paper's conclusions, not just its wording. They are recorded here rather than buried.

  1. The reduction \(k=p(1-p)V\) was applied inside a competitive entry game, where the solo-optimal width is not a best response. It is reinstated as \(k=p(1-p)A\) against the effective reward, and Section 3 closes the resulting loop. The referee's counterexample is reproduced in Section 4.
  2. “Free entry produces two attempts” is withdrawn. Even in the committed-width game the claim required selecting the symmetric mixed equilibrium: for \(p<2-\sqrt2\) any three designated entrants form a pure equilibrium, and the companion paper's own potential selects three. At fixed \(n\) the mixed equilibrium gives \(2n/(n-1)\), not 2. Under endogenous widths the entrant count is not pinned at all.
  3. “Doubling the purse does almost nothing” is withdrawn: it contradicts \(p=1-\zeta/\sqrt V\), under which \(dp/d\log V=(1-p)/2\) and doubling the purse moves \(p\) from 0.01 to 0.29996. The logarithmic-width result applies far above threshold with positive discounting, which is not where the argument was made.
  4. The Millennium “revealed prize” tables are withdrawn along with the conclusion that the purses are scoreboards. The dials were stipulated, so nothing was estimated; and the diagnostic used — purse over prize — is the wrong ratio, because the model's own arithmetic keeps the surplus small in level whenever attempts are long shots. Section 6 now says what can and cannot be said, which is less.

Two smaller repairs: the exposure for the solution hazard is at most \(6\times26+3=159\) problem-years, not \(7\times26\), since the solved problem stopped being exposed in 2003; and the old robustness sentence was arithmetically impossible, since \(k\le V/4\) means a one-million-dollar purse can rationalise an attempt costing at most 250k.

Scope.

Sections 2–5 are proved for the lottery allocation of the companion paper — all entrants pay to completion, one winner is drawn among the successes — with undiscounted, driftless attempts. The first-solution race, in which costs cease when a rival announces and the rival hazard is a function of calendar time, is not solved here; it is the companion's coupled system and it remains open. Section 6 contains no estimation.

2. The price of a success probability

Fix one researcher and one approach. While working, the researcher observes \(dX_{t}=\sigma\,dW_{t}\) with \(\sigma>0\), starting at \(x_{0}\); first passage to a target \(b>x_{0}\) is a solution; working costs \(c>0\) per unit time; abandonment at a floor \(a<x_{0}\) is irreversible. Write \(d=b-x_{0}\) for the distance still to travel and \(L=b-a\) for the continuation width, and define the hardness index \(\zeta^{2}=c\,d^{2}/\sigma^{2}\) — the cost rate times \(d^{2}/\sigma^{2}\), which is exactly the diffusive time for the state to cover the remaining distance.

Proposition 1 (The price list). For any width \(L>d\), the attempt's undiscounted success probability and expected cost are

\[ p=1-\frac{d}{L},\qquad k=\frac{c\,d\,(L-d)}{\sigma^{2}}=\zeta^{2}\,\frac{p}{1-p}. \]

Hence \(k(\cdot)\) is a strictly increasing, strictly convex function of \(p\) on \([0,1)\) with \(k(0)=0\) and \(k(p)\to\infty\) as \(p\to1\); and an observed pair \((k,p)\) determines \(\zeta^{2}=k(1-p)/p\) with no reference to the prize, the discount rate, the rival hazard, or whether \(L\) was chosen optimally.

Proof. Optional stopping applied to \(X\) and to \(X_{t}^{2}-\sigma^{2}t\) at \(\tau=\tau_{a}\wedge\tau_{b}\) — legitimate because \(X\) is bounded on \((a,b)\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]<\infty\) — gives the gambler's-ruin identities \(p=(x_{0}-a)/(b-a)=1-d/L\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]=(x_{0}-a)(b-x_{0})/\sigma^{2}=d(L-d)/\sigma^{2}\), so \(k=c\mathbb{E}[\tau]\). Substituting \(L=d/(1-p)\) gives \(k=c\,d^{2}p/(\sigma^{2}(1-p))=\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)\). Convexity and the limits are immediate. □

Everything else in the paper is an optimisation against this price list.

Corollary 1 (Optimal attempt size). Let \(A>0\) be the expected reward conditional on solving. The attempt maximising \(Ap-k(p)\) over \(p\in[0,1)\) has

\[ p=\Big(1-\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt A}\Big)_{+},\qquad k=p(1-p)A,\qquad \text{value}=Ap^{2}=\big(\sqrt A-\zeta\big)_{+}^{2}, \]

and the attempt is worth mounting exactly when \(A>\zeta^{2}\). Expected spend never exceeds \(A/4\), with equality only at \(p=1/2\).

Proof. \(Ap-\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)\) has derivative \(A-\zeta^{2}/(1-p)^{2}\), which vanishes at \((1-p)^{2}=\zeta^{2}/A\) and is decreasing, so the stationary point is the maximum when it lies in \((0,1)\), i.e. when \(A>\zeta^{2}\); otherwise the derivative is negative throughout and \(p=0\). Substituting \(1-p=\zeta/\sqrt A\) into \(k(p)\) gives \(k=\zeta p\sqrt A=p(1-p)A\), and \(Ap-k=Ap^{2}=(\sqrt A-\zeta)^{2}\). Finally \(k/A=p(1-p)\le1/4\). □

For a researcher with no rivals, \(A=V\), and this is the first draft's Proposition 1. The whole of the trouble in that draft was the step of holding \(A=V\) inside a game where the prize is shared.

Corollary 2 (The surplus is a small multiple of one attempt). If the attempt is optimally sized, the reward exceeds the walk-away threshold by

\[ A-\zeta^{2}=k\,\frac{2-p}{1-p}\;\in\;[2k,\infty), \]

which equals \(2k\) in the long-odds limit and \(3k\) at \(p=1/2\).

Proof. \(A=\zeta^{2}/(1-p)^{2}\), so \(A-\zeta^{2}=\zeta^{2}(1-(1-p)^{2})/(1-p)^{2}=\zeta^{2}p(2-p)/(1-p)^{2}\), and \(k=\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)\). □

Corollary 2 is the paper's most consequential line and its least expected. However enormous the reward, if attempts are long shots then the reward only just clears the threshold, and it clears it by about twice the cost of a single attempt. A prize that looks like a rounding error against the level of the reward can be a large fraction of the surplus that makes attempting worthwhile at all. Section 6 is where the first draft got this backwards.

A verification.

A committed, seeded Euler simulation — given only the boundary location and the increment law — reproduces Corollary 1. At \(A=100\), \(c=\sigma=1\), \(d=4\), so \(\zeta=4\), \(L=10\) and \(p=0.6\), fifty thousand paths at \(dt=2\times10^{-3}\) give \(\hat p=0.6051\) (s.e. 0.0022) and \(\hat k=24.05\) (s.e. 0.092) against \(p=0.6\) and \(k=p(1-p)A=24\). The upward bias in \(\hat p\) is Euler overshoot of the upper boundary and shrinks with \(dt\). The price identity is checked against \(k\) computed from the width at five widths, with residuals at \(10^{-14}\).

3. Equilibrium attempt size

Take the companion paper's lottery: \(n\) researchers attempt the same problem, each pays for its own attempt to completion, and if several succeed one is drawn uniformly for the prize \(V\). Researcher \(i\) chooses a width, equivalently a success probability \(p_{i}\in[0,1)\), and pays \(k(p_{i})\). Choosing \(p_{i}=0\) is not entering. Successes are independent. Conditional on solving, researcher \(i\)'s expected reward is

\[ A_{i}=V\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{1+K_{-i}}\Big] =V\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{j\ne i}\big(1-p_{j}+p_{j}z\big)\,dz, \]

using \(1/(m+1)=\int_{0}^{1}z^{m}dz\), where \(K_{-i}\) counts successful rivals. Payoffs are \(A_{i}p_{i}-k(p_{i})\), so by Corollary 1 each researcher's best response is \(p_{i}=(1-\zeta/\sqrt{A_{i}})_{+}\) and depends on rivals only through the scalar \(A_{i}\). In the symmetric case this collapses to \(A(p)=V[1-(1-p)^{n}]/(np)\).

Proposition 2 (Symmetric equilibrium, finite pool). \(A(p)\) is strictly decreasing in \(p\) on \((0,1]\), with \(A(0^{+})=V\) and \(A(1)=V/n\), and strictly decreasing in \(n\). If \(V\le\zeta^{2}\) the only symmetric equilibrium is \(p=0\). If \(V>\zeta^{2}\) there is a unique symmetric equilibrium \(p_{n}\in(0,1)\), namely the root of \(p=1-\zeta/\sqrt{A(p)}\); it is strictly decreasing in \(n\), and each researcher earns \(A(p_{n})p_{n}^{2}>0\).

Proof. Write \(w=1-p\). Then \(\partial_{p}[(1-w^{n})/p]\) has the sign of \(g(p)=npw^{n-1}-1+w^{n}\), and \(g(0)=0\) with \(g'(p)=-n(n-1)pw^{n-2}<0\), so \(g<0\) and \(A\) is strictly decreasing in \(p\). For monotonicity in \(n\), \((n+1)(1-w^{n})-n(1-w^{n+1})=1-(n+1)w^{n}+nw^{n+1}\) vanishes at \(w=1\) with derivative \(n(n+1)w^{n-1}(w-1)<0\), hence is positive for \(w<1\), giving \(A_{n}>A_{n+1}\). Now \(\Phi(p)=(1-\zeta/\sqrt{A(p)})_{+}-p\) is continuous and strictly decreasing on \([0,1]\) — where the positive part is active it is \(1-\zeta/\sqrt{A(p)}-p\) with \(A\) strictly decreasing, and where it is clamped it is \(-p\) — with \(\Phi(0)=1-\zeta/\sqrt V\) and \(\Phi(1)=-\zeta\sqrt n/\sqrt V<0\). If \(V\le\zeta^{2}\) then \(\Phi(0)\le0\) and \(p=0\) is the unique root, and it is genuinely an equilibrium since a deviator facing \(A=V\le\zeta^{2}\) has best response \(0\). If \(V>\zeta^{2}\) then \(\Phi(0)>0>\Phi(1)\) and strict monotonicity gives exactly one root, which decreases in \(n\) because \(A_{n}\) does. □

Proposition 3 (Large pool). Let \(n\to\infty\) with \(np\to y\). Then \(A\to V\,G(y)\) with \(G(y)=(1-e^{-y})/y\), strictly decreasing from 1 to 0, and the equilibrium condition becomes

\[ G(y^{*})=\frac{\zeta^{2}}{V}, \]

which has a unique positive root whenever \(V>\zeta^{2}\). At that root the expected number of solvers is \(y^{*}\), the solve probability is \(1-e^{-y^{*}}\), the effective reward collapses to \(A^{*}=\zeta^{2}\), individual effort and payoff vanish, and aggregate effort equals the expected payout, \(\lim n\,k(p_{n})=y^{*}\zeta^{2}=V(1-e^{-y^{*}})\). With a setup cost \(K>0\) payable on entry, the same computation gives

\[ G(y^{*})=\frac{V_{e}}{V},\quad V_{e}=\big(\zeta+\sqrt K\big)^{2},\quad p^{*}=\frac{\sqrt K}{\zeta+\sqrt K},\quad N^{*}=y^{*}\,\frac{\zeta+\sqrt K}{\sqrt K}, \]

where \(V_{e}\) is the purse at which a lone researcher exactly breaks even, \(A^{*}=V_{e}\), each attempt costs \(k=\zeta\sqrt K\), and \(N^{*}\to\infty\) as \(K\downarrow0\).

Proof. \((1-p)^{n}=(1-y/n)^{n}\to e^{-y}\) gives \(A\to VG(y)\), and \(p=1-\zeta/\sqrt A\) with \(p\to0\) forces \(\zeta/\sqrt{A^{*}}\to1\), i.e. \(A^{*}=\zeta^{2}\) and hence the boxed condition; uniqueness is strict monotonicity of \(G\). Then \(k=p(1-p)A\to0\) and the payoff \(Ap^{2}\to0\), while \(nk\to y\,A^{*}=y^{*}\zeta^{2}=V\,y^{*}G(y^{*})=V(1-e^{-y^{*}})\). With a setup cost, free entry requires \(Ap^{2}=K\); substituting \(p=1-\zeta/\sqrt A\) gives \((\sqrt A-\zeta)^{2}=K\), so \(\sqrt{A^{*}}=\zeta+\sqrt K\). Then \(p^{*}=1-\zeta/(\zeta+\sqrt K)\), \(k=p^{*}(1-p^{*})A^{*}=\zeta\sqrt K\) and \(N^{*}=y^{*}/p^{*}\). Finally \(V_{e}\) is the purse solving \((\sqrt V-\zeta)^{2}=K\), the lone researcher's break-even condition. □

What is pinned is solutions, not attempts.

The expected number of solvers \(y^{*}\) and the solve probability depend only on \(V/V_{e}\). The number of entrants does not: it is \(y^{*}(\zeta+\sqrt K)/\sqrt K\), which diverges as the setup cost vanishes, because with free entry a great many researchers each buy a vanishing probability. Any claim about how many people attempt a problem is a claim about setup costs, not about the prize.

Competition is competed away exactly.

\(A^{*}=V_{e}\): in a large pool the effective reward equals the purse at which a lone researcher would exactly break even. Every researcher behaves as though facing no competition and a prize of \(V_{e}\), and aggregate effort equals the expected payout. This is complete rent dissipation, and it is the analogue of the classical free-entry result with the cost-probability relation derived rather than assumed.

The fixed point is one dimensional.

Best responses depend on rivals only through the scalar \(A_{i}\), which is why Proposition 2 is a one-dimensional root and not a system. That is the substantive gain from Proposition 1.

4. The committed-width benchmark, and why it fails

The first draft studied a different game: attempt widths committed at the solo-optimal \(L=\sigma\sqrt{V/c}\), with only entry chosen. That game is not the equilibrium, for a reason worth displaying.

The solo width is not a best response.

Take \(V=100\), \(c=\sigma=1\), \(d=5\), and two entrants both using the solo width \(L=10\), so \(p=1/2\) each. Then \(A=V(1-p/2)=75\) and the payoff at the solo width is \(75\cdot\tfrac12-25=12.5\). The best response is \(L=\sigma\sqrt{A/c}=\sqrt{75}\approx8.6603\), worth \(13.3975\): a gain of \(0.8975\), or 7.2%. Competition shortens attempts, and any model that fixes their length misprices it.

Even that game did not deliver two attempts.

Suppose widths are committed anyway, so \((p,k=p(1-p)V)\) are exogenous. Then a set of exactly three entrants, with everyone else out, is a pure Nash equilibrium whenever \(p<2-\sqrt2\approx0.5858\): each incumbent earns \(V[1-(1-p)^{3}]/3-p(1-p)V=Vp^{3}/3>0\), while a fourth would earn \(Vp^{2}(-\tfrac12+p-\tfrac{p^{2}}{4})<0\). Two entrants is never a pure equilibrium, since the third always gains \(Vp^{3}/3>0\) by joining. The companion paper's exact potential selects \(m=3\) for every \(p\) up to about 0.5. So the first draft's “two attempts” was the symmetric mixed equilibrium and nothing else; the coordinated outcome of the same game is three. At fixed \(n\ge4\) the mixed equilibrium gives \(nq\to2n/(n-1)\) as \(p\downarrow0\) — 2.667 at \(n=4\), 2.041 at \(n=50\) — reaching 2 only in the pool limit. The claim is withdrawn.

5. Purse design

A funder commits escrowed capital \(B\) to identical problems with break-even purse \(V_{e}\), choosing the number \(M\) of problems and posting \(V=B/M\) on each; free entry then determines each problem's solve probability. Write \(H(y)=1-e^{-y}\), so \(G=H/y\).

Proposition 4 (Optimal purse). Under Proposition 3, \(V=V_{e}\,y/H(y)\), so \(M=B\,H(y)/(V_{e}y)\) and the expected number of problems solved is \(\Psi=M\,H(y)=(B/V_{e})\,H(y)^{2}/y\). Over the continuous relaxation of \(M\), \(\Psi\) has a unique positive maximiser, characterised by

\[ e^{y}=1+2y, \]

at which, exactly, \(V^{*}/V_{e}=y^{*}+\tfrac12\), \(\mathbb{P}(\text{solved})=2y^{*}/(1+2y^{*})\) and \(\Psi^{*}=(B/V_{e})H(y^{*})^{2}/y^{*}\) — numerically \(y^{*}=1.25643121\), \(V^{*}=1.75643121\,V_{e}\), \(\mathbb{P}(\text{solved})=0.71533186\) and \(\Psi^{*}=0.40726438\,B/V_{e}\) on \(M^{*}=0.56933627\,B/V_{e}\) problems. The optimal purse is a fixed multiple of the break-even purse, independent of \(B\).

Proof. \(G(y)=V_{e}/V\) gives \(V=V_{e}y/H(y)\) and hence \(M\) and \(\Psi\). Maximising \(\log\Psi=2\log H-\log y+\text{const}\) gives \(2H'/H=1/y\), and with \(H'=e^{-y}\) this is \(2ye^{-y}=1-e^{-y}\), i.e. \(e^{-y}(2y+1)=1\), i.e. \(e^{y}=1+2y\). That equation has exactly one positive root, since \(e^{y}-1-2y\) is convex with value 0 and derivative \(-1\) at \(y=0\) and tends to \(+\infty\). At the root \(H=1-1/(1+2y)=2y/(1+2y)\), so \(y/H=(1+2y)/2=y+\tfrac12\). □

For comparison, running the same program through the committed-width model of Section 4, where \(V=V_{\min}(y/H)^{2}\), gives \(e^{y}=1+\tfrac32y\), \(y^{*}=0.76268856\), \(V^{*}=(y^{*}+\tfrac23)^{2}V_{\min}=2.04305637\,V_{\min}\) and \(\mathbb{P}(\text{solved})=0.53358923\). Both constants are confirmed against a two-million-point brute-force maximisation of the exact objective; the first draft's \(9/4\) and two thirds were an approximation to the second of these, evaluated at \(p=1/3\), where the long-odds approximation it relied on is not valid. The general form is \(e^{y}=1+(1+1/\text{power})y\), where the power is 1 when widths adjust and 2 when they are committed.

Two caveats. Integer \(M\) reintroduces budget-dependent rounding, so budget independence is a property of the relaxation. And expected count solved is one objective among many: a funder who cares most about the hardest problem should not spread at all.

5.1 What a purse buys

The first draft asserted that purses buy breadth much more cheaply than depth, and that doubling a purse does almost nothing. The second half is false and the first is not a general truth.

Near the threshold the purse is powerful.

For an attempt sized against reward \(V\), \(p=1-\zeta/\sqrt V\), so \(dp/d\log V=(1-p)/2\) and doubling \(V\) sends \(p\mapsto1-(1-p)/\sqrt2\). From \(p=0.01\), doubling the purse gives \(p=0.29996\); a tenfold purse gives \(0.68694\). The sensitivity is largest exactly where \(p\) is smallest, because small \(p\) means \(V\) only just exceeds \(\zeta^{2}\). In equilibrium the same steepness appears in Proposition 3: near threshold \(y^{*}\approx2(V/V_{e}-1)\), so the expected number of solvers is proportional to the excess of the purse over break-even, and the optimal purse sits at 1.76 times break-even, in the steep region by construction.

What survives of the depth argument.

The companion paper's continuation width at discount-plus-hazard \(\rho>0\) is \(L=\sigma(2\rho)^{-1/2}\operatorname{arcosh}(1+\rho V/c)\), which grows like \(\log V\) once \(\rho V\gg c\), against \(\sigma\sqrt{V/c}\) at \(\rho=0\). That is a statement about attempts run far above threshold under positive discounting. It does not license any claim about the marginal value of a purse near threshold, and it was misused to make one.

6. The Clay problems: what can and cannot be said

Nothing in this section is empirical, and the first draft was wrong to present it as though it were. There is one datum with real content and one theorem with real bite; the rest is arithmetic on stipulated numbers, which is why the tables are gone.

The datum: the purse is not the prize.

Perelman's preprints settled the Poincaré conjecture in 2002–2003; the million-dollar purse was awarded in 2010 and declined. A prize refused by the only person entitled to it is not what was being pursued. The reward for settling a famous problem is priority, the historical record, and whatever a career is worth, and it is not denominated in escrow. That the reward exceeds the notional purse is an observation. By how much is not measured anywhere, by anyone, including here.

The theorem: the surplus is small even when the reward is huge.

Because \(V\) is unobserved, the model cannot predict Millennium effort. What it can do is say which ratio would settle the question, and Corollary 2 says it is not the obvious one. However large the reward, the amount by which it exceeds the walk-away threshold is \((2-p)/(1-p)\) times the cost of one attempt — about twice it for long shots. So the diagnostic for whether a cash purse changes behaviour is purse / 2k, where \(k\) is the expected cost of one serious attempt, and not purse over prize. The first draft computed the second ratio, found it near one percent, and concluded that the purses were scoreboards. That inference does not follow, and under the model it is probably wrong: a purse that is one percent of the level of the reward can be half of the surplus, because the model's own arithmetic makes the surplus a multiple of a single attempt's cost rather than a fraction of the reward.

To see the size of the issue, and purely as arithmetic on round numbers: if a serious attempt costs 1M then purse/2k = ½, and deleting the purse from the reward roughly halves the equilibrium number of solvers, from \(y^{*}=0.0403\) to \(0.0203\); if an attempt costs 10M the ratio is 0.05 and the effect is a five-percent reduction; if an attempt costs 200k the purse is decisive, in that without it the reward falls below the walk-away threshold and nothing is attempted at all. Which of those is the right order for a Millennium problem is not known to me, and the model cannot supply it. The honest output is the ratio to go and measure.

An order of magnitude on rivalry.

Here the arithmetic belongs to the companion paper's first-solution race, not to the lottery of Sections 3–5: there a rival's solution destroys the option, so an attempt faces \(\rho=r+h\) and the share of impatience due to competition is \(h/(r+h)\). One solution since 2000 over an exposure of \(6\times26+3=159\) problem-years gives \(\hat h=0.0063\) per problem-year, and the exact Poisson interval for one event is \([1.6\times10^{-4},\,3.5\times10^{-2}]\). Two corrections both push the same way: the observed problem-level hazard is the aggregate, including the focal researcher, whose own contribution must be removed — with \(N\) symmetric attackers the rival hazard is \((N-1)/N\) of it — and any positive discount rate enters the denominator.

What would change it.

The model localises the whole of the interesting variation in \(\zeta=d\sqrt c/\sigma\). An attempt run at machine cost lowers \(c\) and, if the search is genuinely broader, raises \(\sigma\); both lower \(\zeta\), which lowers the break-even purse quadratically, raises \(p\), and raises the expected number of solvers. If that ever compresses expected time-to-solution from decades to a few years, \(h\) rises by two orders of magnitude and the rivalry share flips from under a tenth to over four fifths, and only then does the clay-pigeon metaphor of the title become the right one. That is a forecast, and it is the one part of the paper that could be checked against something.

7. What this gets wrong

  • The lottery is not the race. Sections 3–5 solve the lottery: everyone pays their attempt to completion and a winner is drawn among the successes. In the first-solution race, costs cease when a rival announces, the rival hazard is a function of calendar time, and the equilibrium is the companion paper's coupled stopping and forward-distribution system, which is characterised there and solved nowhere. The two games differ in a way that matters — in the race the option is destroyed, in the lottery only diluted — so the constants here should not be quoted for a race.
  • Only symmetric equilibria are proved unique. Best responses fall in rivals' success probabilities, so this is a game of strategic substitutes and asymmetric equilibria are not excluded. The best-response map is a contraction when \(\zeta|A'(p)|/(2A^{3/2})<1\), which holds at every parameter combination reported here, but that is a check and not a theorem.
  • Pausing does not by itself remove the threshold. The first draft claimed that allowing a pause, as the companion's intensity extension does with \(u=0\), would remove the sharp walk-away threshold. It would not. With progress frozen while idle, constant discounting and rival hazard, and no information arriving, waiting strictly destroys value — the value function simply decays at rate \(\rho\) — so the optimal control is bang-bang and the work-or-stop boundary is unchanged. Resumption after decades requires something to change while the problem sits: new tools lowering \(c\) or raising \(\sigma\), or information arriving from elsewhere. That is a model in which \(\zeta\) drifts down over time, which is exactly the mechanism of the previous section, and it is the honest way to write down a mathematician who comes back to a problem in twenty years.
  • Difficulty is common, not private. The product survival formula behind \(A_{i}\) and behind the rivalry share needs independence across researchers. In mathematics the dominant uncertainty is shared: whether the statement is true, whether the method can work at all. A rival's visible failure is evidence about one's own prospects, which turns this into a game with filtering and correlated states.
  • Progress is partly public, and credit is not winner-take-all. Preprints and talks reveal rival states, which is a joint-state game. Partial results are published and rewarded, so the all-or-nothing payoff overstates the waste. Verification also takes years — the Poincaré purse was paid seven years after the preprints — so a claimed proof that later fails restarts the race and the hazard conflates “someone claims” with “someone solves”.
  • And there are no data. No parameter in Section 6 is estimated. The cost of a serious attempt and its success probability would both have to be measured before any of this touched the world, and measuring them is harder than the mathematics here.

8. Related work

Racing for a prize.

Loury (1979) and Lee and Wilde (1980) set the pattern: a flow of R&D spending buys a Poisson hazard of success, and free entry dissipates the rents. Dasgupta and Stiglitz (1980) added industrial structure, Reinganum (1981) the dynamic game, and Fudenberg, Gilbert, Stiglitz and Tirole (1983) preemption and leapfrogging. Harris and Vickers (1985, 1987) put the race on a state space with observable positions and studied who gives up. What this paper adds is a derived rather than assumed relation between the cost of an attempt and its success probability: Proposition 1 is a price list, not a specification, and it is what makes the equilibrium of Section 3 a one-dimensional fixed point.

Private progress and preemption.

Hopenhayn and Squintani (2011) is the closest neighbour: privately observed progress, an option to stop, and preemption. Bobtcheff, Bolte and Mariotti (2017) model the tradeoff between publishing early and polishing under priority risk. Moscarini and Squintani (2010) and Keller, Rady and Cripps (2005) study experimentation when rivals' information is a motive to keep going. Those papers keep the strategic loop closed in the harder allocation — first past the post — and leave the cost-to-probability mapping in reduced form. This one closes the mapping in the easier allocation.

Contest design.

Tullock (1980) and Baye, Kovenock and de Vries (1996) price rent dissipation; Taylor (1995) and Che and Gale (2003) design research tournaments; Fullerton and McAfee (1999) auction entry to select contestants; Halac, Kartik and Liu (2017) design contests when the technology must be discovered. Proposition 4 is a design result with an unusually crude instrument — how many purses, and how large — and its answer is a fixed multiple of the break-even purse.

Entry games and potentials.

Morgan, Orzen and Sefton (2012) and Fu, Jiao and Lu (2015) study endogenous entry with subsequent effort; the companion paper's fixed-attempt lottery and its exact potential sit in the tradition of Rosenthal (1973) and Monderer and Shapley (1996). Section 4 uses that potential against the first draft of this paper.

Budgeted races.

The forward–backward architecture of the companion paper is borrowed from the budgeted Brownian race, where one controller prunes many paths under a path-time budget and is paid the terminal maximum. There an optimality condition with a shadow price closes the loop; here a consistency condition between stopping rules and the reward they share. Its concavity certificates say nothing about equilibrium, a point the companion makes and this paper inherits.

9. Closing

An attempt on a hard problem has a price list, \(k(p)=\zeta^{2}p/(1-p)\), and it is remarkably informative: it makes the cost-probability relation a theorem rather than a specification, it reduces the competitive equilibrium to a one-dimensional fixed point in which the effective reward is competed down exactly to the break-even purse, and it delivers an exact optimal purse at 1.7564 times that break-even level. It also says something uncomfortable about the empirics: the surplus that makes a long shot worth attempting is about twice the cost of one attempt, whatever the reward's level, so a purse cannot be dismissed as a rounding error just because it is one.

The first draft of this paper dismissed the Millennium purses on exactly that reasoning, and it was wrong to. The honest position is that the reward is unobserved, the cost of a serious attempt is unmeasured, and the ratio that decides the question is the purse against twice that cost, not the purse against the reward. What is left to prove is harder than what is left to measure: existence and selection for the first-solution race's coupled equilibrium, whose allocation rule — the option destroyed rather than diluted — is the one the title's metaphor actually describes, and which nobody has solved.
